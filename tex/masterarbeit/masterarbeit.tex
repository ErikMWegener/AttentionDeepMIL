	\section{Introduction}
		The problem is simple: what do you do if you want to count wheat ears in a field but no one offers up 
		their time to annotate thousands of ears in hundreds of images? Correct, spend many more hours trying 
		to develop a method that can count wheat ears without any instance level annotations, obviously.

		That basically summarises the origin of the topic for this thesis and presents the motivation for its developed methods,
		although in a very simplified way. Leaving the casual wording aside, it contains a serious and relevant question, 
		which matters not only for plant breeding but also for research fields outside of agricultural science.

		Counting objects in images is a common problem in computer vision and relevant in many fields of application, 
		such as crowd counting or cell counting. In agricultural science, counting plant organs like wheat ears is an 
		important part of phenotyping and yield estimation, as the number of ears per area is one of the central components 
		of grain yield and a recurring trait in plant breeding \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018, madecEarDensityEstimation2019}. 
		Manual counting in the field is laborious and prone to error, especially when many plots have to be compared 
		\cite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019, guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}, which is why image-based counting methods have been developed for this task 
		in recent years. 

		One of the biggest difficulties of these methods is the annotation of the training data with bounding boxes or 
		point annotations, which the fully supervised state-of-the-art methods require. Counting is difficult not only because 
		of the large number of annotations, as a single image can contain more than 10,000 ears 
		\cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}, but also because it is nearly impossible to create correct annotations. 
		Overlap and occlusion make it difficult to see all ears, so that even experienced observers need to count several 
		times to reach a reliable result \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}. In addition, models trained on one 
		dataset often do not transfer to images from other locations, sensors or growth stages, so new conditions 
		frequently require new annotations, which was one of the reasons for creating large shared datasets such as 
		\ac{gwhd} \cite{davidGlobalWheatHead2021}.

		Considering these problems, a method that works without these annotations, or with far fewer of them, sounds 
		rather appealing. This is the starting point of this thesis, which investigates whether wheat ears can be counted 
		with a model that is trained without instance-level annotations, and which performance such a method can reach.

		The idea for the method presented in this thesis comes from a figure in the paper by 
		\textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018}, whose model also forms the basis of this thesis. The model is based 
		on \ac{mil} and assigns a weight to each of the instances that make up a bag. Figure~\ref{fig:mnist_example} shows 
		an example bag of \ac{mnist} patches, in which each patch is shown together with the attention weight computed by 
		the model. The red marks point to the patches with the highest weights, which are all positive patches, with 
		positive being defined as showing the digit 9. In this example, the two classes of patches, 9 or not 9, could 
		simply be separated by a threshold on the weights. Such a separation would then allow the positive patches to be 
		counted based only on the bag label, without any instance annotation.

		This thesis proposes two methods that apply this idea to bags of wheat patches. The first one is based on the 
		\ac{abmil} by \textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018} and counts the positive patches of a bag by applying 
		a threshold to the attention weights. The second one is based on \ac{clam} \cite{luDataEfficientWeakly2020} and 
		adds a patch classifier that is trained on pseudo-labels derived from the attention weights, so that the positive 
		patches are counted from its probabilities instead. Both models are trained only with binary bag labels, which 
		state whether a bag contains wheat ears or not. The counting itself is done in two steps: first, the positive 
		patches of a bag or image are counted, and second, this patch count is converted into the number of wheat ears 
		with a function that is fitted on a small number of annotated images.

		Based on this idea, the thesis addresses the following research questions:
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Can the attention weights of an \ac{abmil} model that is trained only on binary bag labels be used to 
			count positive patches, and what determines the counting error?
			\item What limits the separation of positive and negative wheat patches under bag supervision, and can an 
			additional patch-level task trained on pseudo-labels improve it?
			\item How well can the resulting patch counts be converted into wheat ear counts on real drone images?
		\end{enumerate}

		The work on these questions went through two phases. The first phase focused on counting the positive patches, 
		based on the assumption that once positive patches can be identified with a high enough accuracy, counting the 
		actual wheat ears becomes much easier. The second phase converted the patch counts into ear counts on annotated 
		drone images, where a better recognition of the positive patches did indeed improve the counting performance 
		within one patch definition. The main contributions of this thesis are:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item a counting rule for \ac{abmil} that thresholds the attention weights, together with an analysis on 
			\ac{mnist} and \ac{gwhd} showing that the counting error is mainly determined by the combination of attention 
			activation and threshold, and that on real wheat images the separation of the patches, not the bag 
			classification, is the bottleneck,
			\item an adaptation of \ac{clam} to binary bags with top-k and quantile-based pseudo-labels and a counting 
			threshold calibrated on the validation split, which improves the patch separation without using patch labels,
			\item the transfer of the method to drone images and a conversion from patch counts to ear counts, evaluated 
			on annotated drone images, including an analysis of the influence of the patch size.
		\end{itemize}

		The thesis is structured as follows. The next section summarises the scientific background on wheat ear 
		counting, its challenges, fully supervised counting methods and weakly supervised approaches based on \ac{mil}. 
		The fundamentals section then introduces \ac{mil}, attention, \ac{abmil}, \ac{clam} and the evaluation metrics 
		used in this thesis. Afterwards, the datasets and the construction of the bags are described, followed by the 
		proposed counting method and the evaluation protocol. The experiments are presented largely in the order in which they were carried out, from 
		the validation on \ac{mnist} over the \ac{gwhd} dataset and the \ac{clam}-based model to the drone images and 
		the conversion to ear counts. The last section summarises the results and gives an outlook on future work.

	\section{Scientific Background}
		In order to give an overview of the scientific background of this thesis and to explain its relevance,
		the following section will first introduce the importance of wheat ear counting followed by a discussion of the 
		challenges associated with this task. Subsequently, the current state of the art in fully supervised counting methods 
		will be presented, before finally introducing methods most closely related to the methods proposed in this thesis,
		namely weakly supervised counting methods based on \ac{mil}. 

		\subsection{Wheat ear detection and counting}
			The grain yield of cereals can be described as the product of multiple area-based yield components --- 
			plant density, number of fertile ears per plant, grain count per ear and mass per grain \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}.
			Ear count per ground area plays a prominent role among the mentioned components, being regarded as one of the 
			central agronomic yield components for grain yield in cereals \cite{fernandezWheatEarCounting2018} and 
			the most important index on its own \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}.
			Moreover, ear density is closely linked to yield-determining characteristics such as stand density 
			and tillering \cite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019}.

			Beyond pure yield estimation, ear count is a recurring trait in plant breeding and phenotyping. 
			The number of ears per ground area is one of the adaptive traits that are of interest in agronomy 
			and breeding \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018, madecEarDensityEstimation2019}.
			Ear density allows the characterisation of the tillering capacity of genotypes, as determined by the tillering coefficient, 
			which is calculated as the ratio of the number of ears per unit area to the plant density.
			Counting and density estimation are core building blocks for high-throughput phenotyping because of the quantitative nature of many phenotypical traits.
			Plant density therefore receives increasing attention in breeding programs\cite{jinEstimatesPlantDensity2017a, luCaoTasselNetV2F2020} 
			as do count and localization tasks, which are of similar importance for agricultural production \cite{karamiAutomaticPlantCounting2020}.

			Recent papers have shown that the reliable extraction of these traits or features from image data is possible. \textcite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019}
			used high-resolution RGB images to determine ear density, achieving a similarly high heritability ($H^2\approx 85\%$), comparable to manual counting in the field. 
			This suggests a possible replacement of manual methods by automatic ones. The required spatial resolution of 0.3 mm is achievable with \ac{uav} platforms and 
			is suitable for the phenotyping of larger experimental sites. Practical use cases for stand counting can be found during every stage of agricultural production, 
			from seeding to the yield estimation \cite{luCaoTasselNetV2F2020}. These methods can deliver early information about the need for potential reseeding 
			and an estimate of yield potential \cite{khakiHighthroughputImagebasedPlant2022}.

			The major bottleneck lies in feature collection. Manual counting in the field is widely regarded as laborious, subjective, 
			prone to error and inefficient \cite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019,luCaoTasselNetV2F2020}, especially when the data has to be collected 
			over many parcels \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}. Exactly this effort motivates the development of automated, image-based 
			ear-counting methods, which come with their own methodological difficulties.

		\subsection{Annotation and counting challenges}
			Wheat ear counting is difficult for a multitude of partly related reasons. At its core lies the overall stand density. 
			Compared to other crops, cereals are sown more densely, with a density of 200 to 600 ears per square metre, leading to only partly visible ears in image patches \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}.
			These dense scenes are characterised by strong occlusion and small object sizes leading to a difficult analysis. Occlusions stem from two sources: 
			occlusion by objects that are not part of the scene and the mutual occlusion of similar objects. The latter is the core of the problem \cite{zhangApplicationsDeepLearning2020}. 
			In extremely dense tillering, the overlap is so strong that even experienced observers need to count multiple times to achieve a reliable result \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}. 
			Depending on the occlusion, single ears can be hidden by leaves or neighbouring ears or multiple ears can visually merge into one \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}.

			This overlap has a direct impact on the choice of method. Detection-based approaches typically rely on 
 			non-maximum suppression, which struggles to separate objects that overlap heavily \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}; as an alternative, regression-based counting 
			methods have become established for dense, highly overlapping scenes \cite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019}. Nevertheless, regression is not without its limitations either: 
			in cases of severe overlap and image blurring, significant underestimations occur, and the wheat ears then become almost impossible to distinguish, even for human experts 
 			\cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}.\\

			This is compounded by the high variability of the target object. The colour, size and shape of wheat ears vary greatly and unevenly across the different stages of development, 
			and perspective distortions caused by the angle of capture further exacerbate the differences in size \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}. 
			In breeding populations, this variability is amplified by differences between genotypes, growth stages and the origin of the ear — main shoot or tillering shoot — 
			which produces differences in morphology \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}. The growth stage influences countability in two ways: in later stages, the 
			increasing leaf mass leads to greater overlap between neighbouring plants and makes their classification more difficult \cite{jinEstimatesPlantDensity2017a}, which is why high counting accuracies 
			are achieved primarily in early stages where there is no overlap and no weeds \cite{garcia-martinezDigitalCountCorn2020}.

			In addition to the object itself, the imaging conditions also pose challenges. Changing light conditions during a single flight, a complex background, 
			and the aforementioned variations in shape and colour are among the key difficulties faced by image-based solutions under real-world field conditions \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}. 
			Flight altitude also has a direct impact: as altitude increases, counting accuracy decreases from around 96 \% at 40 m to approximately 76 \% at 60 m \cite{neupaneDeepLearningBased2019}.
			In addition, a spatial resolution better than approximately 0.4 mm is a prerequisite for a reliable density estimate. 
			The timing of the acquisition can also be chosen earlier to minimise overlaps \cite{jinEstimatesPlantDensity2017a}.

			However, the key challenge in this work concerns annotation. Traditional detection-based methods require instance-specific labels (Figure~\ref{fig:annot_spek}) --- such as bounding boxes ---
			which are hardly feasible to generate given the typical number of objects in wheat images: a single image may contain more than 10,000 ears of wheat, 
			which makes the annotation of bounding boxes nearly impossible \cite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019}. Even where deep-learning models in principle facilitate counting, 
			the generation of training data remains very labour-intensive \cite{guoAerialImageryAnalysis2018}. This thesis addresses this problem by using only binary bag 
			labels instead of instance-level annotations and examines how far such labels can support counting under these conditions.

			\subsection{Fully supervised approaches}
			Fully supervised counting of wheat ears can be divided into five methodological categories, which differ in annotation effort, output representation and robustness to occlusion.
			The first is the most widely spread family in the cereal domain and uses detection-based methods for counting. Objects are located by bounding boxes and the count is a by-product of detection.
			\textcite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019} compared the Faster-R-CNN detector with the TasselNet regression network for ear density estimation 
			from high-resolution RGB nadir (top-down) images and found that, under calibrated conditions, both approaches achieved a \ac{rrmse} of around six percent, 
			although Faster-R-CNN remained more robust at advanced growth stages because the ears and background appear differently as the plants mature. 
			For their Faster-R-CNN model, Madec et al. reported an average precision of 0.85, an \ac{rrmse} of 5.3 \% and a coefficient of determination of 0.91.
			With the publication of the \ac{gwhd} dataset by \textcite{davidGlobalWheatHead2021}, detection-based wheat head recognition was standardised. In their 2023 publication,
			they analysed the winning models from the 2020 and 2021 Global Wheat Challenges \cite{davidGlobalWheatHead2020,davidGlobalWheatHead2021} --- predominantly EfficientDet and YOLO variants --- and reported that the 
			winning solutions achieved an average domain accuracy of around 0.7, while the resulting estimate of the number of ears across all test data achieved a coefficient of determination of 
			$R^2$ = 0.814 with an \ac{rrmse} of 0.185 \cite{davidGlobalWheatHead2023}. The authors themselves emphasise that this counting performance falls short of that achieved in specialised studies such as  
			\textcite{madecEarDensityEstimation2019} (\ac{rrmse} = 0.06) and \textcite{sadeghi-tehranDeepCountInFieldAutomatic2019c} (\ac{rrmse} = 0.11).
			More recent wheat-specific detectors are pushing these figures even further: YOLOv7-MA \cite{mengYOLOv7MAImprovedYOLOv7Based2023} achieves an average precision of 93.86 \% on \ac{gwhd} 2021 at 
			35.93 frames per second, while the drone-optimised YOLOv8-FDA \cite{linYOLOv8FDALightweightWheat2025} combines a \acl{map} (\acs*{map}@0.5) of 84.9 \% with a model size of just 2.96 MB 
			and a counting accuracy of over 97.5 \% for cross-row segmentation. The advantage of detection-based methods lies in the additional localisation of each object; 
			their disadvantage is the high annotation effort required for bounding boxes, as well as the decreasing accuracy in the case of dense, overlapping wheat ears.

			The high annotation effort is heavily reduced when using point annotations instead of bounding boxes. The move from bounding boxes to point annotations was formulated by 
			\cite{riberaLocatingObjectsBounding2019} with a weighted Hausdorff distance, a loss function that
			estimates object positions within any fully convolutional network without the need for bounding boxes, region proposals or a specification of the maximal object count.
			\textcite{songRethinkingCountingLocalization2021} improved on that concept with their P2PNet, which directly predicts object positions as point proposals and uses a hungarian matching 
			to set optimal training objectives. On ShanghaiTech Part A, they achieve a \ac{mae} of 51.96. These methods significantly reduce the annotation workload, 
			but still require spatially localised point annotations. Both methods still fall into the family of detection-based methods with the
			difference of using a weaker form of annotation.

			The second family, density-map-based regression, does not detect individual instances. \textcite{lempitskyLearningCountObjects2010} first formulated counting as an integral over a density 
			map learnt from point annotations and introduced the \ac{mesa} loss function, which can be efficiently computed using a maximum subarray algorithm. This paradigm was further developed 
			in crowd counting by MCNN \cite{zhangSingleImageCrowdCounting2016} and, in particular, CSRNet \cite{liCSRNetDilatedConvolutional2018}; the latter achieves a mean absolute error of 68.2 on ShanghaiTech Part A using dilated 
			convolutions and is frequently used as a baseline for wheat ear density maps. In agricultural image processing, the TasselNet family extended this concept to plant organs: 
			while TasselNet \cite{luTasselNetCountingMaize2017} was developed for maize tassels, \textcite{xiongTasselNetv2InfieldCounting2019} extended it to wheat ears with TasselNetv2 using context-augmented local regression, 
			achieving a counting accuracy of 91.01 \% at 13.82 frames per second on the specially collected \ac{wsc} dataset. Density mapping methods require only point annotations and 
			are more robust than detectors in conditions of high object density; however, they do not provide explicit object localisation.

			The third family comprises segmentation-based approaches in which wheat ears are segmented on a pixel-by-pixel basis and subsequently counted.
			SpikeSegNet \cite{misraSpikeSegNetaDeepLearning2020} uses an encoder-decoder network with an Hourglass architecture for this purpose, while \textcite{hasanDetectionAnalysisWheat2018} 
			employed region-based \acp{cnn} for ear detection and analysis under various fertilisation levels. Segmentation captures the object's shape precisely, 
			but requires pixel-accurate masks and is therefore particularly labour-intensive in terms of annotation. 

		 	The fourth family - direct count regression at the image level - dispenses entirely with spatial outputs: \textcite{riberaCountingPlantsUsing2017} regressed the number 
			of plants directly from orthorectified field image sections and achieved a \ac{mape} of 6.7 \% using an Inception-v3 architecture, 
			and WheatLFANet \cite{yeWheatLFANetInfieldDetection2023} adopts a comparable global regression approach with high real-time performance. An instructive special case is WheatNet \cite{khakiHighthroughputImagebasedPlant2022}, 
			which combines a truncated MobileNetV2 with a density and a localisation branch, requiring only point annotations; 
			this places it methodologically between density regression and weak supervision. Overall, there is a trade-off between annotation effort and the information content of the output, 
			which underpins the motivation for weakly supervised methods. However, the figures reported are not directly comparable across studies, as they are based on different datasets, 
			splits and metrics.

		\subsection{Weak supervision with Multiple Instance Learning}
			Weak supervision is a machine learning paradigm that allows models to be trained with limited or imprecise labelled data. In the context of counting wheat ears,
			this means a spectrum of decreasing strength of annotation: from point annotations with just one point per object, through image or bag-level count labels that only
			give the total number of objects in an image, to only image-level class labels that only indicate the presence or absence of objects.

			This kind of supervision is provided by \ac{mil}, which is the core of this thesis. In \ac{mil} an object is viewed as a
			bag of instances and only this bag --- not the individual instances --- receives a label. \textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018} introduced a permutation-invariant attention-
			pooling operator that aggregates weighted instance embeddings into a bag embedding and makes the model interpretable through the attention
			weights. Tested on two real histopathology datasets, they achieve test \acp{auc} of up to 0.968 (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). Since then, there have been several extensions and improvements to this architecture.
			One of them is the work of \textcite{luDataEfficientWeakly2020}, in which they extended the principle with \ac{clam}. It combines attention to identify diagnostically
			important subregions with instance-level clustering for a refined feature space, using only slide-level labels. For the subtyping of renal cell carcinoma, 
			they report a macro-averaged \ac{auc} of 0.973. Both methods were developed for classification rather than for counting.

			In agricultural science, there have so far been only a few weakly supervised counting approaches, and none of them utilise multiple instance learning with bag-level count labels or bag-level labels alone.
			\textcite{ghosalWeaklySupervisedDeep2019} developed an active-learning-inspired framework for counting sorghum ears from drone images, in which a semi-trained \ac{cnn} generates 
			synthetic annotations that are merely corrected by humans. This reduced the annotation time per image from approximately 720 to 180 seconds, with a 
			coefficient of determination of $R^2 = 0.88$ between machine and manual counting. However, this work is detection-based and utilises point or box annotations, 
			rather than bag-level labels. The methodology closest to that of the present work is that of \textcite{wangLearningCountyPixels2025a}, who employ attention-weighted multiple 
			instance learning for county-wide maize yield prediction: here, a county constitutes the 'bag' and individual satellite pixels constitute the 'instances', 
			with the attention mechanism mitigating the problem of mixed pixels; in the best year (2022), a coefficient of determination of $R^2 = 0.84$ is achieved 
			with a \ac{rmse} of 0.83. This approach also differs fundamentally from that of the present work, as it performs a continuous yield regression on satellite pixels 
			rather than counting plants from drone image patches. A literature search in August 2026 did not find any published work that combines attention-based MIL or 
			CLAM with drone image patches and bag-level labels for wheat ear counting.

	\section{Fundamentals}
		To give a comprehensive overview of the fundamental methods and concepts used in this thesis, the following section will first introduce the 
		underlying principles of \ac{mil} and Attention, before explaining the specifics of their combination in attention-based \ac{mil}. Finally, after
		summarising the expansion of attention-based \ac{mil} to \ac{clam}, the section concludes with a listing of the evaluation metrics used in this thesis.
		\subsection{Multiple Instance Learning}
			\acf{mil} is a machine learning paradigm that allows models to be trained with limited or imprecise labeled data. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:mil_ex}, groups of instances get one collective
			label instead of an assigned label to each individual instance. These groups are called bags and are defined as $X = {x_1,...,x_K}$ where X is the 
			bag of size K and $x_k$ are feature vectors of instances. Only the bag label $Y$ is observable while the instance labels $y_k$ are hidden. 
			The bag label is defined as $Y = max(y_1,...,y_K)$ meaning that a bag is positive if at least one instance is positive and negative if all instances are negative \cite{dietterichSolvingMultipleInstance1997}.

			This standardized assumption is not broad enough for some applications because it derives the bag label based a single instance. Therefore, \textcite{weidmannTwoLevelLearningMethod2003} 
			generalized the assumption to a hierarchie of three assumptions. Firstly, the presence-based assumption follows the standardized assumption only expecting the presence of instances of every
			relevant concept. Secondly, the threshold-based assumption assumes a positive bag when the number of positive instances exceeds a minimum. 
			Finally, the count-based assumption stipulates both a minimum and a maximum number, and thus presupposes that it is not mere presence, but the number of instances belonging to the concept, 
			that carries the information determining the label. The counting solution in this thesis explicitly relies solely on the standardized assumption, as the goal is to see what the maximum 
			counting performance is that can be achieved with minimal annotation effort.

			The other decision of importance when solving a \ac{mil} problem is how the discriminative information is modelled and aggregated. For the modelling \textcite{amoresMultipleInstanceClassification2013}
			distinguishes here between an Instance Space paradigm, where a classifier is trained on the instance level whose outputs are aggregated to a bag level prediction, and
			an Embedded Space paradigm, where a bag is mapped to a single feature vector of fixed dimensions and used to train a standard classifier.
			The former assumes that the relevant information can be traced back to individual instances, while the latter shift it to the aggregated bag representation.
			This distinction is important for the following sections because Attention-based \ac{mil} operates on the embedding level while \ac{clam} introduces an additional branch on instance level.
			The classical \ac{mil} literature has produced a range of methods for both paradigms. 
			%including Diverse Density and its extension EM-DD, distance-based 
			%approaches such as Citation-kNN, as well as the maximum-margin formulations mi-SVM and MI-SVM 
			%(Maron and Lozano-Pérez 1998; Wang and Zucker 2000; Zhang and Goldman 2001; Andrews et al. 2003).\\

			With the emergence of deep neural networks, the focus shifted from hand-designed distance metrics to learned representations. An important factor here is 
			the choice of aggregation operator, which combines the instance representations into a bag representation and is commonly referred to as \ac{mil} pooling. 
			Common operators include the maximum operator—which can be interpreted as a direct implementation of the standard assumption—the mean operator, 
			and probabilistic variants such as \textit{noisy-or} \cite{maronFrameworkMultipleInstanceLearning1997} and \textit{noisy-and} \cite{krausClassifyingSegmentingMicroscopy2016}. \textcite{wangRevisitingMultipleInstance2018} demonstrate empirically 
			that embedded-space approaches for bag classification are superior to instance-space approaches. However, what all the operators mentioned have in common is 
			that they are fixed and contain no learnable parameters, meaning they cannot adapt to the structure of the task in question. The solution to this problem 
			is provided by attention based pooling \ref{sec:attmil}.

			
		\subsection{Attention}
			Before examining the specifics of attention-based pooling, it is important to understand the general concept of Attention.
			In its basic form, attention computes an output as a weighted sum over a set of elements, where the weights
			are learned and represent the relevance of each element of the input. Because these weights are not predetermined, 
			but are generated by a differentiable, normalised function, the operator can be trained end-to-end and 
			adjusts the aggregation to the dimension of the operators input.
			
			The Attention mechanism was first introduced by \textcite{bahdanauNeuralMachineTranslation2016} in response to a structural 
			weakness of the encoder-decoder architecture in machine translation. They identified fixed-length context vector, into which the encoder must compress
			the entire input sequence, as the bottleneck. Rather than relying on a single vector, their model computes a separate context vector 
			$C_i$ for every output step $i$ as the weighted sum of all hidden encoder-states $h_j$:
			
			\begin{align*}
				c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} a_{ij}\, h_j, \qquad
				a_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{ik})}, \qquad
				e_{ij} = l(s_{i-1}, h_j).
			\end{align*}
			Here, $e_{ij}$ is an alignment-score that measures how well the operator-input around position $j$ matches to the 
			operator-output at position $i$, $a_{ij}$ is the associated normalised attention-weight, $s_{i-1}$ is the previous decoder-state
			and $h_j$ is the hidden encoder-state at position $j$. The alignment-model $l$ is a small feedforward network, trained together 
			with the rest of the system; and because the score is computed by such an additive network, it is called additive attention.

			Two properties of this formulation are central for their later use. First, the softmax-normalisation makes sure, that the weights 
			are non-negative and sum to one, meaning the context-vector $c_i$ stays in a stable value range independent of the sequence length $T_x$ and 
			it can be represented as a convex combination of the encoder-states. Second, the whole mapping is differentiable, which allows the training 
			of the alignment model through backpropagation, instead of setting it by hand. Additionally, the weights $a_{ij}$ are directly interpretable
			as the contribution of the input-element $j$ to the output $i$.

			\textcite{luongEffectiveApproachesAttentionbased2015} extended the approach by introducing multiplicative or dot-product attention, which is more computationally efficient.
			At the same time, they distinguished between global attention and local attention, where the former attends to all input positions while 
			the latter attends to only a subset.
			Another fundamental differentiation comes from \textcite{xuShowAttendTell2016} who introduced the concept of soft and hard attention. 
			Soft attention is fully differentiable and the weights are calculated deterministically, while hard attention stochastically samples a single input region and is 
			dependent on reinforcement learning for training. In this thesis, only soft attention is relevant because it allows end-to-end backpropagation.

			The best-known use of attention today is the Transformer \cite{vaswaniAttentionAllYou2017}. For this thesis, only one property is important: 
			attention computes learned, normalised weights over a set of elements, which show how much each element contributes to the output. 
			This makes attention usable as a pooling operator in MIL.

		\subsection{Attention-based MIL}\label{sec:attmil}
			To fix the limitations of \ac{mil} mentioned above, \textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018} propose to model the aggregation as an attention 
			mechanism. They describe their input as "a neural network-based permutation-invariant aggregation operator that corresponds to the attention mechanism" 
			and highlight that its application "provides insight into the contribution of each instance to the bag label" \cite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018}. 
			A \ac{mil} model needs to be permutation-invariant because no assumption about the order or dependency of instances in a bag can 
			be made. 

			The authors split the model into three steps: a transformation $f$ of the instances into representations $h_k$, 
			a permutation-invariant aggregation to a bag representation $z$  and a subsequent transformation $g$ to obtain 
			the bag prediction $\hat{y}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:attmil_schema}). The theoretical justification for this decomposition is based on the fundamental theorem of 
			symmetric functions, which, as formulated by \textcite{zaheerDeepSets2018}, states that a function on a set is 
			permutation-invariant if and only if it can be represented in the form $\rho(\sum_{x\in X}\omega(x))$ using 
			appropriate transformations $\omega$ and $\rho$. \textcite{qiPointNetDeepLearning2017} provide an analogous 
			approximation using the maximum operator.

			The operator proposed by the authors weights the instance representations $h_k$ from a bag $H=\{h_1,...,h_k\}$ with trained attention weights
			$a_k$ and calculates their sum:
			\begin{align*}
				z = \sum^K_{k=1}a_kh_k, \quad a_k = \frac{\exp\{w^T\tanh(Vh_k^T)\}}{\sum_{j=1}^K\exp\{w^T\tanh(Vh_j^T)\}},
			\end{align*}
			where $w \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times1}$ and $V\in \mathbb{R}^{L\times M}$ are trainable parameters, $\tanh$ is the hyperbolic tangent function and $z$ is the resulting 
			bag representation. $M$ is the dimension of the instance representations and $L$ is the dimension of the attention space. Since the weights are non-negative due to softmax normalisation and 
			sum to one, the operator remains stable and permutation-invariant across varying bag sizes. 
			The attention weights can then be directly interpreted as the input of each instance to the bag prediction,
			which lends the model interpretability missing from rigid pooling operators.

			To compensate for the limited expressiveness of the hyperbolic tangent in its approximately linear region 
			the authors also propose a gated variant that introduces a multiplicative sigmoid branch to introduce a learnable non-linearity:
			\begin{align*}
				a_k = \frac{\exp\{w^T(\tanh(Vh_k^T)\odot \sigma(Uh_k^T))\}}{\sum_{j=1}^K \exp\{w^T(\tanh(Vh_j^T)\odot \sigma(Uh_j^T))\}},
			\end{align*}
			where $\odot$ is the element-wise multiplication, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function and $U \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times M}$ is an additional set of parameters. Both variants 
			are evaluated on synthetic \acs*{mnist}-bags, with each number-patch is an instance in mixed bags, as well as two 
			histopathological datasets, where the whole-slide images are considered as one bag containing patches as instances.
			The model reaches an \ac{auc} of 0.968 on the colon cancer dataset. For this thesis, the most important properties are the trainability 
			and, above all, the interpretability of the attention weights, because it could allow counting of positive patches under the
			right circumstances.

				\caption[Schematic of the \acs*{abmil} model structure]{Schematic of the \ac{abmil} model structure. $K$ is the number of patches per bag, $x_k$ is one instance of a bag,

		\subsection{\acs*{clam}}\label{sec:clam}
			With increasingly larger bags Attention-based \ac{mil} reaches its limits because the bag label represents only a 
			comparatively weak training signal.	This problem is especially prevalent in medical image analysis, where bags, 
			representing whole-slide images, can contain thousands of patches.

			\textcite{luDataEfficientWeakly2020} propose \acf{clam}, which addresses
			this data inefficiency. The authors describe their approach as a method that "uses attention-based 
			learning to automatically identify sub-regions of high diagnostic value in order to accurately classify whole slide, while also utilizing 
			instance-level clustering over the representative regions identified to constrain and refine the feature 
			space" \cite{luDataEfficientWeakly2020}. The model consists of two interlocking components: an attention
			branch for bag classification and an additional instance-level task that regulates the feature space (Figure~\ref{fig:clam_schematic}).

			Architecturally, the patches are first passed through a pretrained ResNet-50 \cite{heDeepResidualLearning2015a} 
			to extract feature vectors. An attention network operates on these representations. In the multi-class variant, 
			this network has class-specific branches, so that a separate weighting of the patches is learnt for each 
			class (Figure~\ref{fig:clam_schematic}). Aggregation follows the principle set out by \textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018}.

			The characteristic extension introduced by \ac{clam} is the instance-level clustering. In the absence of instance labels,
			the model produces pseudo-labels from the attention distribution. The top-$B$ weighted patches are treated as 
			strong in-class evidence for and the $B$ patches with the lowest attention weights as strong out-of-class evidence against the bag label.
			$B$ is set by the hyperparameter \textit{k\_sample}. These pseudo-labels are then used to train an additional
			instance classifier, whose loss shapes the feature space, so that class-relevant and irrelevant regions are better
			separated. As the labels generated in this way are, by their very nature, noisy --- the most heavily weighted tiles 
			do not necessarily correspond to the actual class --- \textcite{luDataEfficientWeakly2020} do not use the standard 
			cross-entropy for this task, but rather the smooth Top-1 \ac{svm} loss proposed by \textcite{berradaSmoothLossFunctions2018}.
			Its margin property reduces the susceptibility to overfitting when dealing with noisy or sparse labels. 
			\textcite{berradaSmoothLossFunctions2018} justify the need for a smoothed formulation on the grounds that the
			loss function “must be smooth and have non-sparse gradients in order to work well with deep neural networks”.

			The authors empirically demonstrate the data efficiency of their approach. For the subtyping of renal cell 
			carcinoma, 25 \% of the training data was sufficient to achieve an average test \ac{auc} of 0.94. Using the 
			whole training set, the \ac{auc} increased to 0.973. At the same time, the attention maps proved to be interpretable
			and the models were found to be robust when compared with independent cohorts.

			\Ac{clam} was developed for the classification of whole-slide images. In this thesis the procedure is adapted to the 
			counting of wheat ears, in which the patches from drone images are treated as the instances. The modifications needed
			to achieve this task are described in Section~\ref{sec:clam_impl}.

			\subsubsection*{Bag classification}
				At the bag level, classification quality is measured by the \emph{accuracy},
				i.e.\ the proportion of correctly classified bags. It is immediately
				interpretable but can be misleading on imbalanced datasets:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} .
				\end{equation}
				To better analyse the cause of misclassifications the \emph{precision}, \emph{recall} (Equation (2)) and \emph{\ac{auc}} 
				can be used.

			\subsubsection*{Instance separation}
				The quality of the instance-level separation is assessed with the standard
				detection metrics. \emph{Precision} is the share of true positives among all
				instances predicted positive and is sensitive to false positives, whereas
				\emph{recall} is the share of true positives among all actual positives and is
				sensitive to false negatives:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad
					\mathrm{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} .
				\end{equation}
				The \emph{F1 score} balances the two as their harmonic mean and is particularly
				informative on imbalanced data:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{F1} = 2 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision} + \mathrm{Recall}} .
				\end{equation}
				Finally, the \emph{\acl{auc}} (\acs*{auc}) summarises the separability
				across all decision thresholds, the \ac{roc} curve plotting the true-positive rate
				(recall) against the false-positive rate. It can be read as the probability
				that a randomly chosen positive instance receives a higher score than a
				randomly chosen negative one; $0.5$ corresponds to random guessing and $1$ to
				perfect separability. The instance-level annotations required for these metrics are taken from the annotated
				evaluation datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:4}.
				These metrics can also be used to support the evaluation of the bag-level classification.

			\subsubsection*{Counting performance}
				The \emph{\acl{mae}} (\acs*{mae}) reports the average absolute deviation between
				the predicted and the true count. It is easy to interpret and stays on the
				scale of the target, but ignores the direction of the error:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lvert y_i - \hat{y}_i\rvert .
				\end{equation}
				The \emph{\acl{rmse}} (\acs*{rmse}) is defined analogously but penalises large
				deviations more strongly by squaring them, which makes it more sensitive to
				outliers:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2} .
				\end{equation}
				A small gap between the $\mathrm{MAE}$ and the $\mathrm{RMSE}$ indicates evenly
				distributed errors, whereas a widening gap signals a higher dispersion of the
				individual errors.

				The \emph{bias} is the signed counterpart of the \ac{mae} and captures the
				systematic direction of the error. A value of zero indicates no systematic
				offset, a positive value a tendency to overestimate and a negative value a
				tendency to underestimate:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{Bias} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}_i - y_i) .
				\end{equation}
				The \emph{\acl{mape}} (\acs*{mape}) expresses the average absolute error
				relative to the true count and therefore allows comparison across datasets of
				different scale, at the price of being sensitive to very small true values:
				\begin{equation}
					\mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lvert\frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i}\right\rvert .
				\end{equation}
				Finally, the \emph{coefficient of determination} measures the share of variance
				in the true counts that is explained by the predictions:
				\begin{equation}
					R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \bar{y})^2} .
				\end{equation}
				A value of $1$ denotes perfect prediction and $0$ a model no better than
				predicting the mean; negative values are possible for worse-than-mean
				predictions. Because it is normalised by the variance of the ground truth,
				$R^2$ depends on the dispersion of the data: at equal absolute accuracy,
				homogeneous datasets yield lower values than heterogeneous ones.

				The metrics above compare the predicted and the true counts directly. When the predicted count 
				is only an intermediate value that is later converted into the target quantity, it is also of 
				interest whether it orders the samples correctly, independent of its scale. This is measured by 
				the \emph{Spearman rank correlation}, which is the 
				Pearson correlation between the ranks of the true and the predicted counts:
				\begin{equation}
					\rho_{\text{S}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_i - \bar{r})(\hat{r}_i - \bar{\hat{r}})}
					{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_i - \bar{r})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{r}_i - \bar{\hat{r}})^2}} ,
				\end{equation}
				where $r_i$ and $\hat{r}_i$ denote the ranks of $y_i$ and $\hat{y}_i$ among all samples and tied 
				values receive the average of their ranks. Without ties, this simplifies to 
				$\rho_{\text{S}} = 1 - 6\sum_{i=1}^{N} d_i^2 / \left(N(N^2-1)\right)$ with $d_i = r_i - \hat{r}_i$. 
				A value of $1$ means that the predictions order the samples exactly like the true counts, $0$ that 
				there is no monotonic relationship and $-1$ that the order is reversed. As $\rho_{\text{S}}$ only 
				depends on the ranks, it is not affected by a systematic offset or a wrong scale of the predictions.

	\section{Method}
		This chapter describes the proposed method for counting wheat ears in drone images as well as all the underlying concepts developed in this thesis. 
		All details regarding the model architectures, the training procedure and the evaluation protocol are described to be referenced in the
		experiments and results chapter.
		\subsection{Wheat ear counting as a \acs*{mil} problem}\label{sec:method_wheat_mil}

			While there are datasets with annotated wheat ears, such annotation only exists for a small subset of drone images, which would not 
			be enough to train a powerful deep neural network. The labels that are easy to create are the bag labels, which are obtained by 
			simply looking at the image and deciding whether it contains a wheat ear or not. This annotation, while useless for detection, lends itself 
			perfectly to the \ac{mil} paradigm. For that purpose, a bag $X$ is defined as a set of patches $\{x_1,.\dots,x_K\}$, which can originate from 
			one or several images. Each bag is assigned a label $Y$ based on the standard \ac{mil} assumption, i.e. $Y = max(y_1,\dots,y_K)$, where 
			$y_k$ is the label of the patch $x_k$. For the annotated datasets, the patch labels are known and can be used to evaluate the 
			instance-level separation, but are ignored for training. 

			For the bag classification task, attention-based \ac{mil} is used as the permutation-invariant aggregation operator. The bag representation is the 
			weighted sum of the instance representations, where the weights are learned by the attention mechanism. These weights represent
			the contribution of each instance to the bag label. If the attention mechanism is able to learn a good separation of the positive
			and negative patches, the positive patches can be counted by applying a threshold to the attention weights. This number can then
			be used as a proxy for the number of wheat ears in the image. This idea is the central hypothesis of this thesis.

			However, even before experimentation, there are three limiting assumptions that need to be made for this method to work. Firstly, 
			each patch needs to be defined as perfectly as possible to contain exactly one wheat ear. Secondly, the patches need to be cut such that individual wheat ears are separated.
			Lastly, the attention mechanism needs to be capable of learning a good separation of the positive and negative patches.
			The first two assumptions cannot be guaranteed, especially for the unannotated drone images, but the third assumption is exactly 
			what this thesis is trying to test. The experiments are designed to determine whether the attention mechanism is capable of learning a good separation 
			of the positive and negative patches, and whether the counting performance is good enough to be used as a proxy for the number of wheat ears in an image.

			Finally, the proposed method has a theoretical label-saturation problem. The binary presence signal of the bag label saturates after 
			one positive patch. A bag with one positive patch and a bag with 50 positive patches will both generate the same learning signal and, when 
			the supervision saturates, the model lacks the gradient needed to separate the different count values.
		
		\subsection{Counting}\label{sec:counting}
			The main counting goal of this thesis is to count the number of wheat ears in a given image. This is a difficult task because the 
			proposed method has no direct access to the number of wheat ears in an image. Therefore, a secondary counting goal is defined,
			which is to count the number of positive patches in a bag. This goal is more general and can be accessed directly through the 
			attention mechanism or the instance-level branch of the models. Most of the thesis is focused on the secondary counting goal,
			with the idea that the primary counting goal can be achieved by first counting the positive patches and then converting this 
			number into a wheat-ear count. However, for the conversion to be possible, the performance of the secondary counting goal
			needs to be as good as possible, which is the main focus of the experiments. Throughout the thesis, metrics for both counting goals are reported;
			metrics regarding the primary goal are denoted as \texttt{metric}$_{\text{ear}}$ and metrics regarding the secondary goal are denoted as \texttt{metric}$_{\text{patch}}$.

			The counting for the secondary goal is done by a thresholding mechanism on the attention weights or instance scores.
			The threshold $\theta$ is either a fixed value or determined by methods defined below. Positive patch counts are then obtained 
			by counting the number of attention weights or instance scores above the threshold: $\hat{c}_{patch} = \sum_k 1_{a_k>\theta}$.
			The fixed threshold depends on the model. For the attention weights of the AbMIL model normalised by softmax the default
			threshold is $1/K$. For the thresholding of the patch scores of the CLAM model two fixed values are reported:
			$1/K$, taken over the AbMIL model for comparison, and $0.5$ as default threshold of the patch classifier.

			The primary counting goal would be equivalent to the secondary counting goal, if it were possible to define the patches 
			such that each patch contains exactly one wheat ear.
			Because of the dense tillering of the wheat plants and the resulting density of the ears, this is not possible.
			The conversion has to be calibrated to find a function that maps the number of positive patches to the number of wheat ears. The two
			tested functions are a linear and an isotonic function \cite{isotonic}. The linear function is defined as $c_{\text{ear}} = \alpha\,c_{\text{patch}} + \beta$, where 
			$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the parameters of the function. The isotonic function is a non-parametric function that is fitted to the data 
			using isotonic regression. Both functions are fitted on the validation set and then applied to the test set, using datasets with three
			different patch sizes (32, 64, 128) to find the best-performing patch size for the counting task. The best-performing patch size is 
			then used for the final evaluation of the method.

			The count on negative bags is set to zero per default, as they should not contain any positive patches per definition. 
			The counting metrics are computed on all bags, including the negative ones. In an application, it is not known beforehand which 
			images or bags contain wheat ears, so the count of a bag is always the result of the bag decision followed by the patch count. 
			A negative bag that is classified as positive therefore produces a counting error that a pure evaluation on the positive 
			bags would hide. At the same time, the correctly classified negative bags lower the average error. 
			This dilution could affect the interpretation, but as the evaluation is kept the same for all experiments and the datasets are balanced between 
			positive and negative bags, the results are comparable throughout the thesis. For the final conversion to true wheat ear counts, this problem
			does not exist, as all evaluated full images are positive.

		\subsection{Attention-based \acs*{mil}}\label{sec:method_attmil}
				The attention-based \ac{mil} model in this thesis follows the architecture presented in Section~\ref{sec:attmil}, proposed by 
				\textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018} and applied only to the secondary counting goal. The main addition to the 
				original architecture (\url{https://github.com/AMLab-Amsterdam/AttentionDeepMIL}) proposed by this thesis is the central counting mechanism, which
				is a simple thresholding of the attention weights to count 
				the number of positive patches in a bag. Additional modifications were made to the backbone and the attention mechanism to 
				improve the counting performance of the model (Figure~\ref{fig:mod_attmil}), as described in the following paragraphs. 
				Both the gated and ungated attention mechanisms were tested.

				The modifications to the backbone are the addition of GroupNorm layers after each convolutional layer to stabilise the 
				training process, a third convolutional layer to increase the capacity of the feature extractor, and a dropout layer 
				to prevent overfitting. GroupNorm was chosen instead of BatchNorm because of the limitation of one bag per batch. These 
				modifications were aimed at increasing the complexity of the model to allow for better learning of the intricate
				patterns of wheat ears compared to the \ac{mnist} dataset used to validate the original architecture (Section~\ref{sec:mnist_val}).

				For the attention mechanism, different activation functions were tested to find the best-performing one for the counting task.				
				Softmax activation is the standard choice for attention mechanisms and is used as the baseline. It normalises the weights 
				so that they sum to one, which makes them permutation- and size-invariant. A problem for the counting task is that the sum of the weights
				is spread more thinly with increasing bag size, increasing the difficulty of separating the positive and negative patches 
				because their weights are closer together. Additionally, positive patches compete with each other for the attention weights,
				which can lead to a normalisation-saturation problem where strong positive patches are weighted too heavily, causing other positives to fall below the threshold.
				This problem grows with bag size and number of positive patches. Sparsemax \cite{sparsemax} and entmax \cite{entmax} are two alternatives to softmax
				that are designed to mitigate these problems. Both also project into the probability simplex and all weights sum to one,
				but allow for sparse solutions, where unimportant patches are assigned zero weight. Entmax is a generalisation of softmax and sparsemax,
				where the degree of sparsity can be controlled by a parameter $\alpha$. For $\alpha=1$, entmax is equivalent to softmax, for $\alpha=2$ 
				it is equivalent to sparsemax, the standard being $\alpha=1.5$. 

				Sigmoid and min-max normalisations are two alternatives that view each attention weight independently and do not normalise to a sum of one.
				This allows for an easier separation of the patches, but the attention mechanism is no longer permutation- and size-invariant. 
				The sigmoid function maps the weights to the range $[0,1]$ and min-max rescales the weights linearly to the range $[0,1]$. 
				Both methods allow for a more direct separation of the positive and negative patches, but are more sensitive to outliers and the distribution of the weights. 
				The sigmoid function is more robust to outliers, while min-max normalisation is more sensitive to them. 
				The problem of the lost size-invariance due to the lack of normalisation to a sum of one is addressed by dividing each attention weight
				by the average attention weight and then applying LayerNorm, ensuring that the weights are properly scaled even though they do not sum to one.


				The standard counting threshold for the attention weights differs between the activation functions. Softmax uses the average attention 
				weight of $1/K$ as the threshold, sparsemax and entmax use 0.0 as default and sigmoid and min-max use the Otsu method \cite{otsu}
				to find the optimal threshold for each bag. The Otsu method is a simple and effective way of finding the optimal threshold for a given distribution of values. 
				It works by maximising the between-class variance of the two classes (positive and negative patches) in the distribution, 
				which is equivalent to minimising the within-class variance. This results in a threshold that separates the two classes as well as possible. 
				
				The loss of the bag classification uses the standard binary cross-entropy loss, which is defined as:
				\begin{align*}
					\mathcal{L} = -\left[Y\log \hat{p} + (1-Y)\log(1-\hat{p})\right] 
				\end{align*}
				with $\hat{p}$ clamped to $[10^{-5},1-10^{-5}]$. Class weighting is not needed because the bag labels are balanced at 50/50 
				between positive and negative bags. The loss is backpropagated using the Adam optimiser with a standard learning rate of $5 * 10^{-4}$ and 
				a weight decay of $10^{-5}$. When using a pretrained backbone, its learning rate was reduced by a factor of 100.
				
				The variable bag size of some datasets is accounted for by the attention mechanism and the batch size of one bag. The batch dimension is removed,
				which makes any padding and masking unnecessary, allowing free variation of the bag size. Attention pooling is permutation- and size-invariant.

					mechanism are highlighted in red and surrounded by brackets. The modifications are split into structural modifications to the

		\subsection{\acs*{clam}-based implementation}\label{sec:clam_impl}
			The second model architecture used in this thesis is based on the \ac{clam} architecture (Section~\ref{sec:clam}) proposed by \textcite{luDataEfficientWeakly2020} 
			and adds an patch-level classification task to the attention-based \ac{mil} model, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:clam_impl}. As in the original \ac{clam} architecture (\url{https://github.com/mahmoodlab/CLAM}), 
			the patch classifier is trained on pseudo-labels generated from the attention weights. For positive bags, the top-$B$ patches (by attention weight) serve as strong positive evidence and the bottom-$B$ 
			as strong negative evidence; for negative bags, the top-$B$ patches serve as negative evidence.. The patch classification task runs in parallel to the attention branch 
			and takes the same feature representations as input. Because the task here is binary rather than multi-class, the standard binary cross-entropy loss is used 
			for the patch classifier, instead of the smooth Top-1 \ac{svm} loss of the original architecture. The full loss is
			calculated as the weighted sum of the bag and patch-level losses, with the weight of each branch being set by the hyperparameter \textit{--bag\_weight}.
			
			The main differences from the original \ac{clam} architecture are the use of the end-to-end trainable default backbone from the attention-based \ac{mil} model --- 
			the modifications of the backbone were not carried over to this model --- instead of 
			the pretrained ResNet-50 and the use of a single branch for the attention mechanism and patch classifier instead of the multi-class variant, since the task is binary.
			The goal of the additional patch-level task is to improve the separation of the positive and negative patches in the feature space
			by forcing the model to directly learn the separation. With the additional learning signal, the backbone can learn 
			more distinct features for the patches. 

			Because the \acs*{clam}-based architecture trains an patch classifier, counting can be done directly by thresholding the patch-level predictions instead of the attention
			weights. This allows for a more direct counting of the positive patches, as the patch classifier is trained to directly predict the patch labels. The problem of the 
			normalisation-saturation as a result of using softmax for the attention weights is also avoided, as each patch is treated independently and does not 
			compete for weights. The patch classifier is also more robust to outliers, as it is trained on the pseudo-labels and not the attention weights directly.

			For thresholding the patch-level predictions, a different approach is used than for the attention weights of the attention-based \ac{mil} model. 
			Instead of using a fixed threshold for all bags or the Otsu method, a threshold evaluation pipeline is implemented that allows comparison of counting rules
			and calculates and persists evaluation metrics for each method. Overall, four different counting rule methods are evaluated in addition to 
			the fixed thresholds $1/K$ and 0.5. First, a simple baseline is calculated by multiplying the proportion of the positive patches over all patches in the training bags (p$_\text{train} \approx 0.25$)
			with each bag lenght K. Second, the Otsu method is applied to each bag individually to find the optimal threshold for each bag. Third, a threshold free variant that sums the patch-scores directly. 
			Fourth, a global threshold is calibrated on the validation set, using a grid of 0.02 steps in the interval \[0.02,0.88\], and applied to the test set. The best-performing counting rule is then used for the final evaluation of the model.

		\subsubsection{Quantile-based pseudo-labelling}
			As an alternative to the rigid top-$B$ pseudo-labeling, a quantile-based approach was tested. Instead of using a fixed number of patches,
			which can be problematic because of the high variance in bag size and number of positive patches, the quantile-based approach uses a 
			fixed quantile of the attention weights to define the pseudo-labels. This means the top-$q$ quantile (\textit{--pos\_quantile}) of the attention weights 
			is used as strong positive evidence and the bottom-$q$ quantile (\textit{--neg\_quantile}) as strong negative evidence. This approach allows for more 
			flexibility in defining the pseudo-labels, as it can overcome the negative patch bias of the top-$B$ approach, and can yield more robust training 
			of the patch classifier.  

			For both methods however, the high variance in the number of positive patches per bag is a problem. This problem is best visualised by examining what happens in the 
			edge cases. When a bag has very few positive patches, the top-$q$ or top-$B$ choices might include negative patches, because there are no more positives to 
			choose from. Similarly, when a bag has only positive patches, the bottom-$q$ or bottom-$B$ choices might include positive patches, because there are no more negatives to 
			choose from. Overall, this means that when choosing the hyperparameters for the pseudo-labeling, a balance needs to be found between minimising the number of 
			false positives and false negatives, while also ensuring that enough positive patches are included to allow the model to learn a good separation. The quantile approach
			should lend itself better to this problem, because it allows a different weighting of the pseudo-positives and -negatives, especially when considering
			that half the bags are negative and do not provide any positive patches. In order to find the best configuration for the pseudo-labelling, 
			a hyperparameter search was conducted over the parameters \textit{--pos\_quantile}, \textit{--neg\_quantile} and \textit{--k\_sample}.

		\subsection{Pipeline for learned grayscale conversion}\label{sec:learned_grayscale}
			In order to further investigate the possible impact of the colour information, a pipeline was added to the existing models that learns a grayscale conversion from the colour images.
			The pipeline consists only of a 1$\times$1 convolutional layer over the colour channels, whose weights are learned during the training process. This way, the 
			model chooses the optimal grayscale conversion for the task. The method used here is unconstrained --- the weights are not required to be non-negative or to sum to one --- 
			which allows the learning of a difference-based contrast enhancement. This is considerably more expressive than a restrictive weighting.
			The convolutional layer is added before the backbone and the weights are initialised with the standard grayscale conversion weights. 
			The inputs are colour patches and the outputs passed to the backbone are the learned grayscale patches. The learned weights are extracted, normalised to one 
			and compared to the standard grayscale conversion weights to determine whether the model learned a different weighting of the colour channels.

		\subsection{Supervised baseline classifier}\label{sec:method_baseline}
			To check for the existence of a learnable patch-level separation, a supervised baseline model was trained on annotated patches.
			The model uses the same architecture as the attention-based \ac{mil} model, but feeds the patch representations directly into a binary 
			classifier instead of the attention mechanism. This allows for a direct evaluation of the achievable patch-level
			separation under a supervised approach and gives an upper bound for the possible separation performance.
			The model is trained with the same hyperparameters as the other models, but with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs. 
			The main evaluation metric is the \ac{auc}, which is calculated on the test set of the annotated patches and reported as the median over 5 seeds.

		\subsection{Hyperparameter search}\label{sec:greedy}
			The hyperparameter search for the backbone of the models in this thesis was done using a greedy search approach. 
			Hereby the hyperparameters are optimised one at a time, varying only the currently tested hyperparameter, while keeping the other hyperparameters fixed. 
			The best value is then used for the search of the next hyperparameter. This greatly reduces the search space and allows for a more
			efficient search. For example, a full grid search over the three in this thesis examined hyperparameters would require $4\times3\times4 = 48$ 
			configurations, while the greedy search only requires $1+3+2+3=9$ configurations instead. Opposed to a random search,
			this approach allows a direct comparison between candidates of each dimension, because runs only vary in one hyperparameter. 
			This pairwise comparison follows the same approach that is used for all experiments in this thesis.



			The searched space consists of three hyperparameters that determine the capacity of the feature extractor: the number of 
			feature maps per convolutional layer (\textit{num\_maps}), the adaptive max pooling resolution (\textit{pool\_size}) and the 
			embedding dimension of the fully connected layer and the output of the backbone (\textit{M}).

			The activation function stays constant during the search and is selected beforehand. Comparisons over different activation functions and 
			structural backbone modifications are done separately in Sections~\ref{sec:gwhd_activations} and~\ref{sec:gwhd_backbones}. 
			The staring points for each hyperparameters shown in the baseline column of Table~\ref{tab:greedy-space} originate from
			the original \ac{abmil} architecture. They are build as an deliberate restriction as the search cannot return to them.

			The search in rounds:
			\begin{enumerate}
				\item \textbf{Base-run}: Training and evaluation of the baseline configuration
				\item \textbf{Cooridinate step}: Hyperparameters are optimised in a set order: feature maps, pooling resolution, embedding dimension. 
				       For each hyperparameter, every candidate is trained that varies from the current one while the others stay fixed on the current best value
				\item \textbf{Replacement}: A candidate replaces the current best only if it significantly improves the current best value for the target metric
				\item \textbf{End of Round}: After no hyperparameters changed in a round, the search is finished otherwise a new round is started with a maximum of 2 rounds
				\item \textbf{Final run}: The best configuration is trained on 5 instead of 3 seeds serving as the final result for the hyperparameter search
			\end{enumerate}

			The idea behind the second round is to check whether a candidate is still optimal after the other hyperparameters have been changed. A second round 
			therefore only costs as many training runs as the number of new combinations created by the first round. Technically, the search starts each experiment as its own trainings process with the existing trainings and evaluation pipeline, using the same code
			as all the other experiments. 
			
			The target function $S$ is a combination of the bag-\acs*{auc} and the patch \ac{auc}, with a respective deduction for seed-dispersion, 
			and is calculated based on the in mlflow aggregated metics:

			\begin{equation}
				S = 0.5\,\mu\!\left(\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{bag}}\right)
					+ 0.5\,\mu\!\left(\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{patch}}\right)
					- 0.3\,\sigma\!\left(\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{bag}}\right)
					- 0.3\,\sigma\!\left(\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{patch}}\right)
			\end{equation}
			
			$\sigma$ denotes the standard deviation and $\mu$ the mean over the three seeds. The same weights for the two \acp{auc} are chosen
			because the bag-level \ac{auc} is a prerequisite for the patch \ac{auc} and the second is the main goal of the search. The deductions
			are done to punish configurations with collapsed seeds.


		\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:eval}

			As presented in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}, the evaluation of the models can also be split into three
			different categories: bag classification, patch-level separation and counting performance.
			
			The bag classification relies mainly on the classification accuracy, with the \ac{auc} as a secondary metric. Precision and recall take a diagnostic
			role in assessing the cause of misclassifications. This is the first evaluation step for 
			each of the models, as it is the main task of the \ac{mil} paradigm and the two subsequent tasks rely on a good performance of the bag classification.
			A model is accepted for further evaluation if it achieves a bag classification accuracy of at least 90\%.
			This criterion was softened for the \ac{clam} experiments, as the misclassification pertains mostly empty bags, which are not relevant for the counting task.
			The patch-level separation is evaluated using the \ac{auc}, with precision, recall and F1-score as diagnostic metrics, to put the \ac{auc} into context.
			For some experiments the \ac{auc} is calculated in two ways: patch \ac{auc} on the raw weights and thresholded patch \ac{auc} on the binarized patch labels after 
			thresholding. The counting performance is mainly evaluated using the \ac{mae}, as it best shows how closely the predicted counts match the true counts.
			\Ac{rmse} and $R^2$ are used as secondary metrics to put the \ac{mae} into context and give better information about the distribution of the errors.
			For the conversion to wheat ear counts (Section~\ref{sec:img_infer}), the evaluation changes in two 
			respects. As every full image contains wheat ears, the counting metrics are calculated on the converted 
			ear counts of the 70 test images, with the mean predictor instead of the always-zero predictor as reference. 
			In addition, the Spearman correlation $\rho_{\text{S}}$ between the hard patch count before the conversion 
			and the annotated number of ears is reported. It shows whether the patch count ranks the images correctly 
			by their number of ears, independent of the fitted conversion function, while the \ac{mae}, bias and $R^2$ 
			of the converted counts also include the error of the conversion. A high $\rho_{\text{S}}$ together with a 
			large \ac{mae} therefore points to a problem of the conversion rather than of the patch count.
			Important to note is that the counting metrics are calculated on all bags while the patch-level metrics are calculated only on those
			bags predicted as positive. Pairwise comparisons over shared seeds are given as wins/seeds with the difference as the median or range if necessary.

			Model evaluation and selection is done on the test set of the respective dataset, while all calibrations of thresholding and conversion functions
			are done on the validation set. The problem of model optimization for the test set that occurs as a result of 
			choosing models based on the best results on the test set should somewhat be mitigated through two practices.
			Firstly, throughout the experiments, models that were optimized on one dataset were then further evaluated on another dataset in order to evaluate different goals. 
			Therefore, the model selection was done on the test set of the first dataset, while the evaluation was done on the test set of the second dataset, preventing 
			the optimization on the test set of the second dataset. Secondly, because calibration of the counting conversion functions is done on the validation set, 
			the final evaluation of the counting performance is done on the test set, which was not used for any optimization or calibration.
			Experiment runs were performed with three different seeds (1, 10, 100) to account for the stochasticity of the training process. 
			For more robust results, reruns of some experiments were performed with 5 seeds (additional seeds 1000 and 10000). 
			Because of collapsing seeds the metrics are reported as median over all seeds.

			The evaluation protocol grew chronologically with the experiments and was not fully defined at the beginning of the thesis. Therefore, some of the earlier experiments
			do not follow the protocol as strictly as the later experiments. Especially since the counting methods were only incrementally developed, some of the metrics
			were only considered in the later experiments, when the goal changed from establishing the plausibility of the methods to optimizing the performance.
			However, the main metrics for each category are reported for all experiments, allowing for a comparison of the results throughout the thesis and showing 
			the improvement of the methods with development. The final evaluation of the method on the drone dataset is done with the full evaluation protocol, 
			including all metrics and multiple seeds.

		\subsection{MNIST dataset}\label{sec:data_mnist}
			The \ac{mnist} dataset \cite{lecunGradientbasedLearningApplied1998} consists of
			grayscale images of handwritten digits from 0 to 9 with a size of $28\times28$
			pixels, split into 60,000 training and 10,000 test images. It is one of the most
			widely used benchmarks for image classification and was used by
			\textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018} to construct the \ac{mnist}-bags on
			which attention-based \ac{mil} was first evaluated. In this thesis, it serves as a
			controlled setting in which every patch can be classified unambiguously, so that
			the behaviour of the aggregation and the counting rule can be examined
			independently of the recognisability of the patches (Section~\ref{sec:mnist_val}).
			Additionally, on the \ac{mnist} dataset the primary and secondary counting goals are
			aligned.

			A bag is formed by randomly sampling digit images, and
			its label is positive if it contains at least one image of the target digit nine
			and negative otherwise. The number of nines in a bag is therefore also the true
			patch count. The training bags are sampled from the \ac{mnist} training images and
			the test bags from the \ac{mnist} test images, so that no digit image occurs in
			both.

			Two kinds of \ac{mnist} bag datasets are used. The first reproduces the bag-size
			sweep of \textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018}. The bag size is drawn from a
			normal distribution with a mean of 10, 50 or 100 and a standard deviation of 2, 10
			or 20, respectively, and training sets of 50, 100, 150, 200, 300, 400 and 500 bags
			are created for each bag size.As the digits are sampled at random and each digit occurs with roughly the same
			frequency, about 10\,\% of the patches in a bag are nines. Short positive bags
			therefore contain only one or a few positive patches, while positive bags of
			length 100 contain about ten.

			The second dataset is designed to reproduce the composition of the wheat bags
			(Section~\ref{sec:bag_construction}) while keeping the perfectly recognisable
			\ac{mnist} patches. Its bags have a size of 100, and the positive bags contain the
			same distribution of positive patches as the wheat bags, with an average of 52.4
			positive patches per positive bag and a standard deviation of 28.6. Across all
			bags, including the negative ones, this results in a mean count of 26.2 and a
			median of zero. This high-density dataset is used in Section~\ref{sec:mnist_density}.


		\subsection{Image datasets}
			Originally, only the drone datasets were planned to be used for the experiments in this thesis. However, the limited number of annotations on those images

			\subsubsection{\acs*{gwhd} dataset}
				For the detection and counting of wheat ears, the \acf{gwhd} dataset 
				\cite{davidGlobalWheatHead2021} is the most comprehensive publicly available dataset. 
				It was created to address the lack of large, diverse datasets for wheat ear detection and counting, which are essential 
				for developing robust machine learning models. The \ac{gwhd} dataset contains around 6,400 images taken at ground level with 275,187 wheat ears annotated with bounding 
				boxes from 12 countries, capturing a wide range of wheat varieties, growth stages, lighting conditions, and camera angles. 
				This diversity makes it an ideal benchmark for evaluating the performance of different detection and counting methods. 
				Additionally, the annotations allow the counting performance of the developed method to be evaluated on a large dataset without annotating a new one.
				The first of two problems posed by this dataset is that
				less than 10 images are true negatives without any annotations. This makes it impossible to define bags image-wise, as the negative bags would be underrepresented.
				Instead, the patch-pooling method described above was applied, with patches defined by their overlap with the bounding boxes. The other problem is the 
				strong domain shift between the images of the different splits. It is the result of the different acquisition conditions between institutes and their disjunct separation between the splits.
				Using those splits would add an additional level of difficulty to the already difficult counting 
				task. Therefore, new splits were created where each split received a distinct percentage of the patch pools relative to its bag number. For most of the datasets created with a 
				train/val/test split of 1000/200/200 that meant that $1000/1400*100\approx71\%$ of the patches were assigned to the training set and $\approx14\%$ to the val and test split respectively. 
				The patch pools were also split to avoid data leakage.
					
			\subsubsection{Real drone images}\label{sec:real_drone}
				The drone images are split into two different datasets. All of them were taken with the RGB camera of a 
				DJI Mavic 3 Multispectral (M3M) in nadir view at a resolution of 5280$\times$3956 pixels.

				The first dataset was collected in 2025 on the DiPredict trial field in Halle, with the wheat in the final 
				growth stage just before harvest. The photographs were taken during three automatic flights under sunny 
				conditions, on 8 July at a height of 5\,m and on 14 and 24 July at a height of 4\,m. As the flights were 
				planned for generating orthomosaics, consecutive photographs overlap, so the same part of the field can 
				appear in several photographs. From these photographs, 173 tiles of 1024$\times$1024 pixels were cut and 
				point-annotated. Similarly to the \ac{gwhd} images, the annotations allow for an instance-level evaluation 
				of the counting performance. The pre-existing train/val/test splits (83/20/70 images) were used.
				The missing true-negative images required the same patch-pooling method as for \ac{gwhd}, applied within 
				the existing splits, with the positive patches defined by the point annotations 
				(Section~\ref{sec:patch_definition}).

				The second set of images was specifically collected for this thesis in 2026 and contains 176 images, taken 
				from a height of 4\,m. Half of the images were collected before the flowering stage as true negatives and 
				the other half after the wheat ears were visible. Both were taken in the same field. This clean separation 
				of the positive and negative images, the fact that the images only include the field section (no background 
				that would need to be excluded in the patch generation process) and the high density of wheat ears in the 
				positive images allow for a simple patch-collection method. Positive patches were cut in a regular grid 
				from the Drone\_BBG\_2 images, while negative patches were cut in a regular grid from the Drone\_BBG\_1 
				images. The resulting patch pools were then used to create the bags by random sampling. A risk of this 
				method is label noise in the positive patches, as there is no guarantee that every positive patch contains 
				a wheat ear.
		
			\subsubsection{Synthetic drone images}\label{sec:data_synth}
				In order to have easy access to a large number of point-annotated images for testing different methods, 
				a synthetic dataset was created using Blender 4.0.2. Each scene covers an area of 0.9$\times$0.9\,m, in 
				which the wheat ears are placed at independent, uniformly sampled positions and with random orientations. 
				Each ear is connected to the ground by a stem, and additional bent stems 
				are placed at random positions to increase the density of the stand. The scenes are rendered in nadir view 
				from a camera at a height of 4\,m with a field of view of $18^\circ$ and a resolution of 1024$\times$1024 
				pixels, similar to the real drone images.
				The point annotation of each ear is placed at its centre. 
				1000 positive and 1000 negative images were generated, which were then used to define the positive and 
				negative patch pools used for random sampling of the bags. The only difference from the method used on the 
				real annotated drone images is that the negative patches were defined on the negative images without any 
				wheat ears. This dataset was only used to test the method for general feasibility, representing an easier 
				problem than the \ac{gwhd} images, because of the clearer separation of plant and background.
		
		\subsection{Patch extraction and labelling}\label{sec:patch_definition}
			The patch definition process varies depending on the annotation type of the images. For images with \texttt{Bounding boxes}, the positive patches
			were defined by centring a regular grid of patches on each bounding box. The grid size is calculated by: 
			\begin{equation}
				\text{Grid Size} = \max(1, \lfloor(\frac{Box Size * (2-Threshold)}{Patch Size})\rfloor).
			\end{equation}
			The equation ensures that at least one patch is defined per bounding box, that the grid size is proportional to the bounding box and 
			that the threshold is taken into account. The default threshold used for this patch definition is 1.0. This method is compared
			to a basic grid-based method in Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_new_splits} where all patches are defined on a regular grid and labelled based on bounding box 
			overlap with a threshold of 0.7. The overlap is calculated as the ratio of the intersection area to the union area of the patch and the bounding box.
			The second method is only used in the mentioned section.
			For images with \texttt{point annotations}, each annotation resulted in exactly one positive patch centred on the point.
			For the \texttt{time-dependent} drone images, the positive patches were cut on a regular grid from the positive images.
			Negative patches were generally defined by a regular grid of determined patch size on either true-negative images or on positive images, under the constraint of 
			no overlap with any annotation. Before saving the patches into the bags, they were converted to grayscale using the standard conversion formula to 
			reduce the complexity of the model. The only exception for this step are the bags created for Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_color}. Here the
			patches kept their three colour channels in order to examine its effect on the training process. In this thesis, three patch sizes were used depending on the experiment: 32$\times$32, 64$\times$64 and 128$\times$128.
			The chosen patch size is mentioned in the respective dataset name.

		\subsection{Bag construction}\label{sec:bag_construction}
			The bag construction process is identical for almost all datasets. Half of the bags of each split were created with a positive label and the other half with a negative label.
			The mean bag size was set to 100 with a standard deviation of 10. The patches for each bag were randomly sampled from separate positive and negative patch
			pools for each split to avoid data leakage. The number of positive patches in the positive bags was uniformly sampled between 1 and the bag size (mean $\approx 50$), 
			to cover the whole range of densities evenly and test the model across all of them.
			The standard bag split was 1000/200/200 for train/val/test.
			Because the number of images was sufficient and the purpose of this thesis was to test the general feasibility of the method, no data augmentation was applied.
			
			For creating bags per image, used in Section~\ref{sec:img_infer}, each image is cut into patches of predetermined size along a regular grid. 
			Patch labels for instance-level and counting evaluation are assigned based on the 
			already established overlap rule with the annotations if given. In the use case of this thesis, the patches are positive if they 
			contain at least one annotated wheat ear.

	\section{Experiments and results}
		The experiments follow the order of the questions they answer, which largely
		corresponds to the order in which they were carried out. Each section states its
		goal, reports the results and ends with the conclusion that motivates the next step.
		The chapter is divided into four parts. Section~\ref{sec:mnist_val} validates
		attention-based MIL on MNIST, where the patches are perfectly recognisable, and
		uses this setting to separate the two sources of counting error: the recognition of
		the positive patches and the counting rule. Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_results} transfers the model
		to the GWHD dataset and identifies the patch level as the bottleneck.
		Section~\ref{sec:clam_results} 
		introduces the CLAM-based model with an explicit patch-level training signal and
		optimises its pseudo-labelling and counting rule. Finally,
		Sections~\ref{sec:drone} and~\ref{sec:img_infer} 
		transfer the selected configurations to the drone images and convert the patch
		counts into wheat ear counts.
		\subsection{Method validation on \acs*{mnist} dataset}\label{sec:mnist_val}
			Before the method is applied to wheat images, it must be demonstrated that
			the underlying architecture actually works.  Additionally the \ac{mnist} experiments help to separate two possible sources of error: recognising the positive patches 
			and the counting rule that reads them from the attention weights. On the wheat data both are mixed. On \ac{mnist} the 
			patches are recognised almost perfectly, so remaining counting errors can be attributed to the counting rule. 
			To this end, the bag-size sweep from the original publication is reproduced on \ac{mnist}. The
			dataset is suitable for this purpose because its patches can be classified individually and unambiguously: it is possible to determine without error
			whether a digit is a nine. Any weakness that becomes apparent here is therefore due to the
			aggregation method and not to the recognisability of the patches.

			This section is divided into five experiments: the validation of the bag
			classification against published results (Section~\ref{sec:mnist_bag}), the
			counting performance on the MNIST patches
			(Section~\ref{sec:mnist_count}), the comparison between gated and ungated attention
			(Section~\ref{sec:minst_gated}), a dataset with a considerably higher and more widely
			distributed density of positive patches, on which two activation functions of the
			attention mechanism are compared (Section~\ref{sec:mnist_density}), and finally an
			analysis of the learnt feature representations (Section~\ref{sec:mnist_features}),
			which serves as the reference for the same analysis on the wheat images.
			
			The experiments use the two \ac{mnist} bag datasets described in
			Section~\ref{sec:data_mnist}. All results are evaluated on separate test bags and,
			for the bag classification, compared with the values reported by
			\textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018}.

			In total, 42 configurations — the combinations of bag size, number of training bags and attention mechanism (gated or ungated) — 
			were trained with five seeds each. Because some seeds, 14 of the 210 runs, collapsed to randomness during training, 
			the distribution of the results over the seeds is two-peaked. Therefore, the median value is reported instead of the mean.

		\subsubsection{Counting proof-of-concept}\label{sec:mnist_count}

			method on the \ac{mnist} dataset. The counting rule defined in Section~\ref{sec:counting} is applied to the trained model:
			for every bag that was predicted as positive, the patches whose attention weight lies above the threshold of $1/K$ are counted, with $K$ as the number of patches
			in the bag. Bags that were predicted as negative get a count of 0. The results over all runs are accumulated in Table~\ref{tab:counting-core}

			The share of exactly correct counts drops from 0.86 to 0.60 and 0.50. As roughly half of the bags are empty and are correctly counted as zero,
			a value of 0.50 for a bag size of 100 corresponds to the statement that practically none of the positive bags are predicted exactly.

			The amount of training data has a significant impact. With a bag length of 100, the
			coefficient of determination $R^2$ rises from 0.066 for 50 training bags to 0.737 for 400 bags, whilst the MAE falls from

			the thresholding.

			The relationship between precision and recall defines the mechanism. Precision rises to 0.99 as the bag size increases, 
			while recall falls to 0.59. What is counted is therefore almost always correct; roughly half is overlooked. The
			threshold is thus set too high. The cause lies in the normalisation of attention: the softmax function constrains the weights of a 
			bag to sum to one and concentrates the weight on a few particularly unambiguous patches. Since the
			training objective is solely the bag decision, there is no incentive to distribute attention evenly across all positive patches. As
			the number of patches increases, therefore, more and more genuine positives fall below the 1/K threshold.

			The counting error on \ac{mnist} is therefore mainly caused by the 1/K threshold and not by the learned features.
			 But it could still be shown that the simple counting rule conceived for this 
			thesis works in principle. The problems of the thresholding will be addressed in later experiments, which will also be transferred to the wheat datasets.

			(patch \ac{auc} higher by 0.005 and 0.016). 

			The ungated variant is used for the subsequent experiments. The rationale
			is not that it is superior, but that, with the same
			training budget, no reliable difference is apparent; in this case, the
			simpler variant with the lower number of parameters is preferable. The comparison is repeated 
			on the wheat images in Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_gated}, where the patch level serves as the selection criterion.

		backbone for the patches of the test bags. They are projected to two dimensions with

		(seeds). As their feature spaces are not aligned, each model forms its own cluster,
		and the separation of the classes has to be judged within each cluster.

		\subsubsection{Interim conclusion}
			The \ac{mnist} experiments show that the implementation reproduces the results of \textcite{ilseAttentionbasedDeepMultiple2018} 
			and that gated and ungated attention perform almost identically on the bag level. 
			They also show three things that matter for the following experiments. Firstly, the difficulty of the task does 
			not disappear as the bag size increases, but shifts from the bag level to the patch level. Secondly, while
			counting on perfectly classifiable patches is possible in principle, the remaining error consists of systematic 
			undercounting covering 95\% to 97\% of the \ac{mae}. The cause of this is the fixed threshold at 1/K and not the learnt
			representation. The recall of the threshold rule largely accounts for the measured bias. Finally, the density test shows that the normalisation of the
			softmax activation itself becomes a barrier at high positive density. The feature analysis confirms this attribution: the backbone learns features in
			which the positive and negative patches are clearly separated, so the remaining
			error arises in the counting rule and not in the representation.

			This last point is important for the following section. On \ac{mnist}, counting is
			limited by the threshold because the patches are reliably recognised individually.
			On the wheat images, this cannot be assumed: if the patches are harder to
			distinguish, a second limitation can add to that of the counting rule. The
			\ac{mnist} results provide the reference that allows the two to be told apart, and
			the high-density dataset, which replicates the composition of the wheat bags,
			connects the two sections.

		\subsection{Attention-based MIL on the \acs*{gwhd} dataset}\label{sec:gwhd_results}
			Section~\ref{sec:mnist_val} showed that the counting mechanism works when the patches are clearly separable, and that the 
			remaining error is caused by the counting rule. On the wheat images, both effects occur together. This 
			section transfers the validated model to the \ac{gwhd} dataset and uses the findings from \ac{mnist} to separate them. 
			The transfer is incremental: architecture,
			optimiser and counting rule are kept as in the MNIST experiments, so that every change
			in performance can be attributed to the dataset and not to the method.

			The section first defines the reference points against which all results are
			measured, and then follows a chain of experiments in which each step builds on the
			result of the previous one:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item the model with naive counting on the original \acs*{gwhd}-splits
				\item the same model on the newly created splits with the two variants of the patch definition
				\item gated instead of ungated attention
				\item alternative attention activation functions
				\item four modifications to the backbone 
				\item a greedy hyperparameter search over the dimensions of the backbone
				\item a dataset with reduced positive patches per bag
				\item colour input and learned grayscale conversion
			\end{itemize}

			None of these steps improves the patch separation noticeably. The section closes with
			an analysis of the learnt feature representations, which shows where the separation
			is lost. The collective results of all experiments
			mentioned in this chapter are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:gwhd_results} in the appendix.

			As long as not mentioned otherwise, all the runs in this section use the same configuration: the attention-based \ac{mil} model 
			with two convolutional layers in the backbone, embedding dimension $M=500$, attention-dimension $L=128$, kernel size of 5, 
			adaptive max pooling on $4\times4$ and 50 feature maps. Training is done over 50 epochs with the Adam optimizer, learning rate 
			$5*10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-5}$ with one bag per optimization step. Five seeds were used in each experiment and each experiment
			changes exactly one parameter to isolate the effect of that parameter on the performance and to make the runs pair-wise comparable. 

			All values in this section are medians across the seeds. In none of the
			experiments did a seed crash. The variation across the seeds is discussed where
			it is relevant.

			A decision must be specified before any results are presented, as it determines how each experiment in this section was evaluated. 
			The selection throughout is based on patch separability; the counting error is regarded as a secondary criterion.
			The reason for this stems from the objective: the number of positive patches is merely an intermediate value and is subsequently 
			to be converted into an ear count. A count that happens to be close to the true value but is based on the wrong patches
			does not support this conversion. The counting error alone does not distinguish between the two cases. The 
			\ac{mae} therefore always has to be interpreted in relation to the patch separability represented by the patch \ac{auc}.

			cannot distinguish the two cases. The \ac{mae} is therefore always interpreted together
			with the patch \ac{auc}.

			\subsubsection{Original and custom splits}\label{sec:gwhd_new_splits}
 
				The first experiments train the default configuration validated in
				Section~\ref{sec:mnist_val} without changes on three bag datasets that differ in
				how the images are split and how the patches are defined.

				sensors, growth stages and lighting conditions are strictly separated. For the
				\textit{custom} splits, all images are merged, permuted image by image and only
				then divided, so that the domains are mixed across the subsets while the patch
				pools remain disjoint. On the custom splits, two patch definitions are compared
				(Section~\ref{sec:patch_definition}): in the \textit{grid} variant, all patches
				are cut on a regular grid and labelled by their overlap with the bounding boxes;
				in the \textit{centred} variant, the positive patches are placed around each
				bounding box. Table~\ref{tab:gwhd_splits} gives the results of all three
				datasets.

				\paragraph{Bag level}
				On the original splits, the bag classifier degenerates. With a recall of 1.000
				and a precision of 0.516, it predicts almost every bag as positive, which results
				in an accuracy of 0.530, far below the acceptance threshold of 0.90. The bag
				\ac{auc} of 0.889 shows, however, that the ranking of the bag scores still works;
				only the decision threshold of 0.5 fails. The test loss confirms this: with 3.22
				to 4.89 compared with 0.41 on the custom splits,
				the predicted probabilities are shifted far to one side. On both custom splits,
				the bag classification reaches an accuracy of 0.910 and 0.900 and passes the
				acceptance threshold. The failure on the original splits is therefore a problem
				of calibration under domain shift: a model whose scores are calibrated on the
				training domains keeps the relative ranking of the bags but loses the absolute
				scale that the bag threshold relies on.
				
				\paragraph{Counting}
				The counting inherits this failure. As patches are only counted in bags
				predicted as positive, a classifier that predicts almost every bag as positive
				assigns a count to almost every empty bag. With an \acs*{mae}$_{\text{patch}}$ of
				25.08, the model on the original splits is slightly worse than the always-zero
				predictor (24.80), and only 3.5\,\% of the bags are counted exactly. On the
				custom splits, the error drops to 13.87 and 15.66, about 40\,\% below the

				same as on \ac{mnist}: with a bias of $-12.99$ and $-13.65$, the ratio
				$|\text{Bias}|/\text{\acs*{mae}}$ reaches 0.94 and 0.87, so almost the entire
				error is a systematic underestimation.

				The patch \ac{auc} is low on all three datasets. On the original splits it
				reaches 0.597, on the custom splits 0.508 (grid) and 0.552 (centred). The domain

				failure on the original splits lies in the calibration of the scores. Compared
				with the 0.909 of the supervised classifier (Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_reference}),
				however, the model reaches only a small part of the achievable separation on any
				of the splits: it recognises that a bag contains wheat ears, but not in which
				patches they are.

				The two patch definitions cannot be distinguished. The difference in patch
				\ac{auc} (0.552 versus 0.508) is smaller than the dispersion over the seeds (0.07

				a separation of the patches that the model does not achieve, the following
				experiments focus on the patch level.

			\subsubsection{Gated attention on \acs*{gwhd}}\label{sec:gwhd_gated}
			As described in the fundamentals section the gated attention mechanism increases the expressiveness of the attention branch by 
			adding a multiplicative gate $\tanh(V) \odot \sigma(U)$. On the \ac{mnist} bags both variants were indistinguishable on the bag-level and
			therefore the ungated variant was chosen for the following experiments. As the patch-level was now identified as the bottleneck this
			decision is re-evaluated for the counting goal on the \ac{gwhd} dataset.
			
			The bag-level performance stays untouched with an accuracy of 0.885 to 0.900 and an \ac{auc} of 0.953 to 0.951. Similarly, the patch-level:
			a patch \ac{auc} of 0.538 to 0.552 with a median-difference over five shared seeds of -0.01. The added gate does not improve the separation between
			the ear and background patches and the expectation for this experiment is not fulfilled. But a secondary observation can be made:
			the gated variant delivers a more stable performance over the seeds with a patch \acs*{auc}-dispersion between 0.520 and 0.561 compared to 0.354 and 0.601 
			for the ungated version. The gated variant is more stable across seeds, but not better.

			The counting performance on the other hand gets worse. The \acs*{mae}$_\text{patch}$ increases from 15.66 to 19.41 with four of the five seeds
			showing worse results. With that the error gets back closer to the reference error of 27.06 and the  $R^2_{patch}$ drops from 0.346 to 0.057 meaning the 
			predictions barely explain the variance above the mean. More insightful is the direction of the deterioration: with -17.89 the bias
			is the most negative up to this point with a ratio $|\text{bias}/\text{MAE}|$ of 0.92. A likely explanation is
			that the gate increases the selectivity of the attention mechanism, making the weight distribution sharper, so that fewer patches lie above the threshold of 
			1/K. The additional expressiveness of the attention-branch worsens the threshold-based counting without improving the separability of the patches.
			
			But the experiment is not without value. It shows that the gated attention mechanism is not productive for the current data and model configuration.

			\subsubsection{Activation functions of the attention mechanism}\label{sec:gwhd_activations}
			Softmax normalises the attention weights to the sum of one leading to a strong competition between the patches. At around 27\% positive patches per bag
			every weight is necessarily small. This suggests that an activation that does not norm to one or an explicitly sparse activation
			could separate the classes better. Five activation functions were tested: softmax, sigmoid on standardised scores, bag-wise min-max normalisation,
			sparsemax and entmax with learnable $\alpha$. 

			Before the results are presented potential confounding factors need to be discussed. The choice of the activation function changes three things
			in parallel. It changes the normalisation of the weights; it changes the pooling, because the bag-embedding is rescaled by the mean of the 
			attention weights and put through a LayerNorm for the non-normalising variants; and thirdly the counting rule. The threshold $\theta$ is chosen
			after the activation: $1/K$ for softmax, 0 for sparsemax and entmax, where the counting is reduced to determine the support size and a 
			bag-wise calculated Otsu-threshold for min-max and sigmoid. The results in Table~\ref{tab:gwhd_activations} therefore do not only
			compare the activation functions but a combination of activation and counting rule. This combination makes the results 
			explainable.

			all carriers of the weight distribution are counted. With a recall of 0.043 only around 4\% of the positive patches are caught and 
			the bias of -25.78 is close to the reference error of 27.06, meaning the counting method returns almost nothing. Entmax
			shows the same pattern in a weaker form with a recall of 0.362 but the second lowest patch precision of the series. Both variants 
			push the $R^2_{patch}$ into negative values, therefore beneath the performance of a trivial mean predictor. 
			Although these arguments only directly speak against the combination of sparse activations and a threshold of 0, the 
			context also allows the ruling out of the sparse activations in general. When the threshold is already set to 0 and the recall is still low,

			no change of the threshold can improve the counting performance.

			falls below 0.5, meaning the error is not dominated by a systematic underestimation. The improvement appears
			where an improved threshold would be expected: the recall increases to 0.594 from 0.395 while the precision stays 
			almost unchanged at 0.802 to 0.824. This version of the counter therefore recognises more positive patches.

			but is still too low for a reliable hard threshold. A positive patch receives a higher attention weight than a negative patch in only about two thirds
			of the cases. Since the counting error improves more than the patch \ac{auc}, part of the improvement probably comes from
			the thresholding and not from the activation itself. To separate the effects the softmax configuration as a baseline was tested 
			again with the Otsu-thresholding. The trained model 
			is the same in both runs and the threshold-independent patch \ac{auc} confirms it, as it is identical for all five seeds up to 
			the third decimal. The only difference is the thresholding.

			The results contradict the expectation. The Otsu-thresholding does not improve the softmax but worsens it significantly. The \ac{mae}
			increases to 24.80, almost reaching the reference error of 27.06, and each of the five seeds is worse, with a pairwise median difference of $+9.13$.
			$R^2_{patch}$ becomes negative, the patch recall drops to 0.077 and the bias of -24.68 gives a relation of almost 1.0 for $|\text{bias}/\text{MAE}|$. 
			The return of the counter is basically nothing.

			The mechanism behind this problem can be concluded based on the logged thresholds. On the softmax-weight Otsu 
			sets the median thresholds to values 2.81 times the average weight, whereas the naive rule lies at a factor 
			of 1.0 by definition; the threshold therefore lies far too high. For the sigmoid outputs the same method sets the 
			thresholds around 0.5, where it sensibly splits the bimodal distribution 
			between (0,1). Softmax, on the other hand, normalises to a sum of one, resulting in a distribution that is narrow and heavily right-skewed — and
			this shape causes a variance-based counting rule to fail.

			With that the advantage of the sigmoid activation cannot be attributed to either one of the modifications but to the combination of both.
			What matters is that the counting rule fits the shape of the weight distribution generated by the activation function. 
			An adaptive counting rule alone is not sufficient, when it is applied to an unfitting distribution.
			Two further observations can be made following this interpretation. First, the min-max activation uses the same Otsu-thresholding
			and, with a \ac{mae} of 15.38, does not profit from it. Its bag-wise dilation to $[0,1]$ distorts the distribution in a different way than sigmoid,
			probably caused by the missing non-linearity of the activation function. Second, the results shown in Table~\ref{tab:gwhd_activations} 
			are redone experiments on five seeds to achieve a more robust result. Earlier experiments on three seeds showed a reversal of the 
			performance between softmax and sigmoid activation. It is therefore not possible to easily name one of the two modifications
			as the better one. But what can be concluded is that simply replacing a rigid threshold with an adaptive one is not sufficient and 
			the problem of finding the best counting rule will be revisited in later sections of this thesis.

			Over all variations so far, the sigmoid activation with Otsu thresholding reaches the highest patch
		    \ac{auc} of this section, 0.655. Even the best activation cannot clearly separate the positive and negative patches and none 
			reaches a point where a hard threshold could work reliably.

			\subsubsection{Backbone modifications}\label{sec:gwhd_backbones}
			As the performance-bottleneck cannot be solved by the parameterisation of the attention branch or its activation alone, the 
			features themselves are the next candidate. The original backbone with a two-layer convolutional network condenses
			a $64\times64$ patch to $4\times4\times50$. To increase the complexity of the feature extractor, with the goal of better
			capturing the complex features of the wheat ears, four modifications were tested. Each modification is testing
			a different assumption: group normalisation for a more stable optimisation, an increased pooling-resolution from $4\times4$ to $8\times8$
			against the loss of spatial details, a third convolutional layer for more abstract features and a dropout layer with $p=0.25$ to prevent overfitting.

			The bag-level shows a saturation effect. For all four variants the bag-\acs*{auc} lies between 0.945 and 0.974 with dispersion between seeds of 0.005; there
			is no more performance to be gained there. The dropout variant has the worst counting performance (\ac{mae} 15.58) while having 
			the best bag \acs*{auc} (0.974). This shows that a better bag \ac{auc} does not necessarily lead to better counting.

			Regarding the counting performance, two variants seem to help slightly. The group normalisation reaches 12.51 and the increased pooling-resolution
			reaches 13.24 \ac{mae} compared to 15.66 from the reference configuration. Both cases are accompanied by an increased \ac{auc} of 0.619 and 0.596 and a halved bias.
			The third convolutional layer worsens the counting performance to 17.38 \ac{mae} and moves the error profile: the bag-precision decreases to 0.789, the
			bag-recall increases to 0.960. More depth therefore makes the model more generous rather than more accurate and the resulting small bias
			of $-2.74$ is, once again, merely a cancellation. Two further experiments show that these differences are not caused by the modifications themselves.

			The first comparison is an earlier series of the same experiments on the same
			dataset, but with a wider model (M = 1024 and 128 feature maps instead of
			500 and 50) and three seeds. There, the reference configuration already achieves an
			\ac{mae} of 12.09, and no backbone variant improves upon it:
			group normalisation achieves 12.49 with 4×4 pooling and 12.50 with 8×8, the
			increased pooling resolution alone 13.57, the third convolutional layer 13.37, and
			dropout 17.61. Since the seeds 1, 10, and 100 are the same in both rows,
			the comparison can be made in pairs, and the corresponding variants end up at
			practically the same absolute level in both rows: 12.49 versus 12.51 for
			group normalisation and 13.57 versus 13.24 for pooling resolution.
			What differs between the rows is not the variant, but the
			reference—12.09 for the wide backbone and 15.66 for the narrower backbone.
			
			The second experiment varies the counting rule instead of the capacity. All four
			backbone modifications were repeated using sigmoid attention and thus the adaptive
			Otsu threshold, while the number of feature maps was kept at 50;
			group normalisation was additionally combined with the increased
			pooling resolution. As Table~\ref{tab:gwhd_backbones} shows, all five
			variants then fall between 11.02 and 12.54, with the unmodified reference at 11.89
			right in the middle of the range. The pairwise median differences range from $-0.61$ to $+1.72$ at
			a level of about 12.00; thus, no intervention makes a difference. The patch level
			also remains unchanged, ranging between 0.406 and 0.648 compared to 0.655 for the reference,
			and no variant improves it.

			However, an adaptive threshold alone is not sufficient: Under Softmax with
			an Otsu threshold, the increased pooling resolution of 17.34 versus 24.80
			makes a significant difference again. Where the selection rule does not
			match the weight distribution, the backbone once again acts as a compensating factor. The modifications
			do not improve the representation; they compensate for a mismatch
			between the shape of the attention distribution and a threshold that cannot
			adapt to it. The third convolutional layer is the worst variant with Softmax (17.38)
			and one of the best with Sigmoid (11.07), outperforming its Softmax counterpart in
			all five seeds.

			modification for which all three selection rules from Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_activations} were successfully done. With
			Softmax and a fixed threshold, it achieves 13.24, thereby improving upon its baseline
			of 15.66; with Softmax and the Otsu threshold, it achieves 17.34 compared to a
			baseline of 24.80, so it also improves, but from a much worse
			starting point; and with Sigmoid and Otsu, it achieves 11.02 compared to 11.89,
			where the improvement is virtually negligible. The patch \ac{auc} is again identical between the two
			Softmax runs, at 0.596. The same architectural
			change is worth $-2.4$, $-7.5$, or $-0.9$ in absolute error, depending on how
			the number is read from the attention weights. The counting rule therefore has a 
			larger influence on the result than the backbone.

			A secondary result from the sigmoid control series is that the bias is no longer consistently negative for the first time. 
			In combination with the increased pooling resolution,
			the bias drops to $-0.50$ with a \ac{mae} of 11.02, a ratio of
			0.05, and in combination with group normalisation and increased pooling, it
			even becomes positive at $+4.61$. Since the individual seeds of the first variant range from $-10.84$
			and $+5.08$, this does not mean that the bias has been eliminated, only that the counting error no longer has a consistent sign.
			A systematic underestimation becomes a seed-dependent over- or underestimation. This
			fits into the overall picture, in which the sign of the counting error is determined by the
			threshold rule and not by the representation.

			When measuring against the criterion of patch separability, this section shows no result. 
			Across all nine backbone configurations the patch \ac{auc} lies between 0.406 and 0.648, which is entirely 
			within the range already achieved by the activation variants. Changes to the backbone architecture do not improve the separation:
			neither more depth, a higher spatial resolution, normalisation nor regularisation improves
			the separation of background and ear patches. All modifications only change the counting error and the error profile
			and both depend more on the counting rule than on the features themselves, as far as that can be concluded at this point.

			varies across all twelve trials only between 0.9346 and 0.9564 and is therefore saturated, as already noted in the preceding sections. The composite

			search identifies as an improvement — feature maps from 50 to 64, pooling from 4 × 4
			to 8 × 8 — falls within this range. Within the explored space, the search found no change that could be distinguished from noise.

			checked one by one. Here, a systematic procedure searches three dimensions
			of the feature extractor simultaneously and over two rounds. The fact that this approach
			fails to raise the patch \ac{auc} above the known range, indicates that the bottleneck does not
			lie in an ill-chosen combination of backbone dimensions.

			The counting \ac{mae} behaves differently from the target measure: it varies across the
			trials from 11.62 to 17.35, and thus shows significantly greater variation than the patch \ac{auc}. This
			is not a weakness of the search, but the pattern which matches the previous experiments:

			range of trials. A supplementary search that optimises the count error could help in that regard, but
			with the patch \ac{auc} remaining constant, the \ac{mae} would be built on a flawed representation and therefore not be a reliable measure of the model's performance.

			target metric from the parent run and thus from the test metrics. The chosen
			configuration is therefore not validated independently. This is an artefact of the already mentioned selection method based on the 
			test metrics instead of the validation metrics. Secondly, the resolution of the
			search is limited by the three seeds — it would not have been able to distinguish
			small real differences from noise. This is irrelevant for the conclusion drawn here,
			as it is based on the range across all trials and not on a single comparison.

			Overall, the greedy search experiments line up with the previous results: The patch \ac{auc} is saturated and cannot be improved any 
			further by modifications to the backbone.

			\subsubsection{Reduced positive density}
			A final effort to improve separability was to tackle the problem from the data side. 
			If around 27 \% of all patches are positive, a bag can already be identified as positive based on
			its coarse texture, and the model never has to locate individual positive patches or concrete wheat ears. 
			Reducing the proportion of positive patches to around 5 \%,
			by ensuring that the number within a positive bag is evenly distributed between one and twenty
			rather than between one and the bag size, makes the \ac{mil} problem more difficult,
			and the expectation was that this would force the model to learn the patches more
			accurately.

			The intervention achieves neither goal. The bag classification drops, with an accuracy of 0.690 and an \ac{auc} of 0.757
			compared with 0.900 and 0.951, meaning that the configuration no longer fulfills the
			acceptance criterion of the evaluation protocol: with five positive patches out of a hundred, the averaging attention pooling loses the
			signal. At the same time, the patch-level performance does not improve, with a patch \ac{auc} of 0.617,
			which falls within the same range as the group normalisation and sigmoid variants on
			the simpler dataset. The patch precision plummets to 0.256, the lowest value in the entire series, meaning that three out of four
			patches classified as positive are actually background; the bag precision of 0.683 also indicates
			furthermore that around one-third of the bags predicted as positive
 			contain no ears at all.

			The counting performance falls below that of the trivial predictor. With patch \ac{mae} = 5.26 compared with a reference error of 4.92, 
			the model is once again worse than the always-zero predictor, with R$^2_{patch}$ = $-0.525$ and an exact hit rate of 0.355. The distribution
			across the seeds is bimodal, with three seeds clustered around 4 to 5 and two at 22
			and 25.

			The most important observation is the sign of the bias: it reverses
			from $-13.65$ in the dense dataset to $+3.29$. An underestimation thus becomes
			an overestimation. The threshold rule remains unchanged, and a fixed threshold
			of 1/K selects an approximately constant number of patches. As long as 27\%
			of the patches are positive, this is too few. At 5 \%, the same
			behaviour is too many. The threshold does not adapt to the target density.

			Overall, the intervention has failed to achieve the hoped effect of forcing the model to learn the two-class features more accurately. 
			Additionally, the bag-level performance is no longer sufficient to meet the acceptance criterion.

			\subsubsection{Learnt feature representation on the \acs*{gwhd} dataset}\label{sec:gwhd_features}
				None of the interventions in this section raised the patch AUC above 0.655, whereas
				the supervised classifier in Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_reference} 
				reaches 0.909 with the same backbone on the same patches. The patches therefore
				contain the information needed to separate them, and the feature extractor has
				enough capacity to learn it. The remaining question is whether the features learnt 
				under bag supervision contain this information and the attention mechanism does not use it, 
				or whether the separation is already missing in the features themselves. To
				answer this, the instance embeddings of the default model on the centred GWHD bags
				are examined in the same way as for MNIST in Section~\ref{sec:mnist_features}.

				\begin{figure}[ht]
					\centering
					\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/feature_vis_gwhd.png} 
					\caption[\acs*{umap} projection for the GHWD embeddings]{\ac{umap} projection of the instance embeddings $h_k$ of the GWHD test
					patches (centred bags), coloured by the true patch label. Embeddings from 5 seeds of one model, each forming its own cluster.}
					\label{fig:umap_gwhd}
				\end{figure}

				Figure~\ref{fig:umap_gwhd} shows a clearly different picture from MNIST. As for \ac{mnist}, each cluster 
				corresponds to one seed. Within each band, the
				positive and negative patches overlap to a large extent and are spread to a
				similar degree. This is plausible given the composition of the
				patches: both classes are dominated by the same background of leaves, stems and
				soil, and the positive patches differ only by the wheat ears they contain. The
				projection is not quantitative evidence, as UMAP is a non-linear projection. It is,
				however, consistent with the low patch AUC of the attention weights.

				Together with the supervised reference, this shows where the limitation lies.
				The separation is already missing in the input of the attention mechanism, which
				explains why no modification of the attention branch or its activation could
				improve the patch level. Since the same backbone learns separable features under
				patch supervision, the limitation does not lie in the data or in the capacity of the
				feature extractor, but in the training signal. At the same time, the bag
				classification remains saturated with a bag AUC of about 0.95. The model can
				therefore decide whether a bag contains wheat ears without learning which patches
				contain them. Once the bag loss is minimised, it provides no further gradient that
				would force the backbone to separate the patches, which corresponds to the
				label-saturation problem described in Section~\ref{sec:method_wheat_mil}.

			\subsubsection{Interim conclusion}\label{sec:gwhd_interim}

			None of the eight interventions in this section achieved the intended improvement of
			the patch level. Taken together, however, they narrow down where the problem lies.

			The bag level is resolved and saturated. Once the domain shift of the official
			splits is removed, the bag accuracy rises from 0.530 to 0.900 and above, and the bag \ac{auc}
			lies between 0.943 and 0.974 across all configurations, with a dispersion over the seeds
			of about 0.005. Only the dataset with reduced positive density falls below the acceptance
			threshold again. The bag performance carries no information about the patch level, as
			the dropout variant shows by combining the highest bag \ac{auc} (0.974) with one of the weakest
			counting results. This justifies selecting configurations by their patch separability.

			The patch level is the bottleneck. Neither the patch definition, gated
			attention, the activation function, the backbone modifications nor the greedy search moves
			the median patch \ac{auc} beyond the range of 0.406 to 0.655, compared with 0.92 to
			0.95 on the \ac{mnist} bags. In the greedy search, the range across all trials is smaller than
			the dispersion of a single configuration across its seeds.

			The counting error is systematic, and its size and sign are determined by the
			counting rule, i.e.\ the combination of attention activation and threshold. With softmax,
			$|\mathrm{Bias}|/\mathrm{MAE}$ lies between 0.87 and 0.94. It only drops where activation
			and threshold match, as with sigmoid and Otsu (0.46). The fixed $1/K$ threshold selects an
			approximately constant number of patches, which is why the bias reverses from $-13.65$ to
			$+3.29$ on the dataset with reduced density.

			The capacity of the feature extractor is not the limiting factor. The backbone
			modifications only appear effective in combination with the fixed threshold and the narrow
			backbone (Table~\ref{tab:gwhd_backbones}); % CHECK (currently Table 11)
			their order changes with the activation, and none of them raises the patch \ac{auc}.
			They shift the attention distribution relative to the threshold rather than improving the
			separation.

			The section therefore separates two distinct limitations. The counting error is a
			read-out problem that can be addressed directly, for instance by a threshold calibrated on
			the validation split or by a soft count. The patch separability, on which any such rule
			operates, remains capped at 0.655 regardless of the attention branch and the capacity of the
			backbone. That the two are distinct is shown by configurations that count better without
			separating better, such as group normalisation with increased pooling and sigmoid, which
			reaches an \acs*{mae}$_{\text{patch}}$ of 12.28 with a patch \ac{auc} of only 0.406.

			The analysis of the learnt features in Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_features} shows where
			the second limitation originates. The supervised reference shows that the patches
			are separable with the same backbone, but under bag supervision the features do not
			separate them, because the saturated bag loss provides no incentive to do so. A
			better counting rule alone can therefore not solve the problem. The feature
			extractor is kept in its default configuration from here on, and the CLAM-based
			model in Section~\ref{sec:clam_results} % CHECK
			adds an explicit patch-level training signal to the bag supervision.

		\subsection{\acs*{clam}-based model}\label{sec:clam_results}

			The CLAM-based model addresses the missing separability of the \ac{abmil} model. It adds a patch classifier on
			a branch parallel to the attention mechanism, trained on pseudo-labels derived from
			the attention weights. The model thus remains weakly supervised, but receives a
			training signal that acts directly on the patch level.

			The experiments in this section again build upon each other:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item the patch-separation of the \acs*{clam}-based model compared to the \ac{abmil} model \\
				\item optimising the top-B pseudo-labeling with the parameters \texttt{k\_sample} and \texttt{bag\_weight} \\
				\item testing the quantile-based pseudo-labeling as an alternative \\
				\item modifying the counting rule \\
			\end{itemize}

			All experiments use the standard backbone of the \ac{abmil} model with the two convolutional layers, 50 feature maps, $4\times4$ pooling,
			$M=500$, $L=128$ and kernel size of 5. This decision is based on the negative results of the previous section, where the 
			backbone modifications did not improve the patch separation significantly. Every difference in performance can therefore be attributed to the
			added patch-level branch. The attention branch follows the original architecture of the \ac{clam} model with the 
			gated attention mechanism and the softmax activation function. The gated attention mechanism did not show any significant changes regarding the 
			patch separation in the previous experiments and is used here to stay close to the original \ac{clam} model. The same reasoning applies to the softmax 
			activation function. Additionally, it has no more influence on the patch separation and counting performance, since the 
			counting task is now performed on the patch scores from the patch classifier. With the centered \ac{gwhd} bags,
			the same dataset as for the \ac{abmil} experiments is used.

			Every configuration was trained for 3 seeds, with the median of the three being reported. Repeated runs on
			5 seeds were not deemed necessary for the \acs*{clam}-based model.

			As already mentioned, the patch scores originate from the probabilities of the patch classifier for the \ac{clam} model,
			compared to the used attention weights used in the \ac{abmil} model. Therefore the evaluation differs in three places:
			\begin{enumerate}
				\item As the patch scores are no longer dependent on their bag, the patch \ac{auc} can be calculated over the whole dataset, instead
				      of excluding the negative bags. The \ac{abmil} evaluation was done only on the bags predicted to be positive.
				\item precision and recall are calculated on a threshold of 0.5 and describe the separation for said value.
				\item Counting metrics are still calculated on all bags, with negative bags receiving a count of 0. The fixed thresholds reported are 1/K (taken from the AbMIL model) and 0.5.
			\end{enumerate}

			The reference point is still the same test split as before. The always-zero predictor has a \ac{mae} of 27.06 and the mean predictor a \ac{mae} of 30.71.

			\subsubsection{Default \acs*{clam} configuration vs. \acs*{abmil}}
			The starting point is the default values from the original \ac{clam}: k\_sample = 8 and
			bag weight $w = 0.7$. In each positive bag, the 8 patches with the
			highest attention are thus labelled as positive. The 8 patches with the lowest attention
			are labelled as negative. In negative bags, the 8 patches with the highest attention are labelled as negative. 
			The bag weight distributes the total loss between the bag and patch-level tasks.

			With the default configuration, the \acs*{clam}-based model reaches a patch \ac{auc} of 0.701 and exceeds the 
			best \ac{abmil} value of 0.655 in all three seeds, while the softmax reference of \ac{abmil} lies at 0.552. 
			Even without any tuning, the additional patch branch therefore improves the separation beyond what was possible with attention-based MIL, 
			as expected from the feature analysis in Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_features}.

			The two values are not computed in exactly the same way. For \ac{abmil}, the patch \ac{auc} was calculated 
			on the positive bags only, whereas for the \acs*{clam}-based model it is calculated on all bags. 
			This difference has little effect on the comparison. The negative patches of both bag types are 
			sampled from the same negative patch pool (Section~\ref{sec:bag_construction}), and the patch scores of the instance 
			classifier do not depend on the bag a patch belongs to. Adding the negative bags therefore 
			only adds more samples from the same negative distribution, which leaves the expected \ac{auc} unchanged. 
			The remaining difference lies in the source of the scores. The attention weights of \ac{abmil} are normalised 
			within each bag, so the same weight can mean different things in different bags. The probabilities of the 
			patch classifier are absolute and directly comparable across bags. The more important difference is that 
			the attention weights are normalised per bag, while the probabilities of the patch classifier are 
			comparable across bags, which is also what a global counting threshold needs.
			On the bag level, the \ac{auc} of 0.952 is saturated like for the \ac{abmil} model, 
			but the accuracy of 0.855 stays below the acceptance threshold.

			At a threshold of 0.5, the precision is 0.810 but the recall only 0.208, meaning that only about a 
			fifth of the positive patches get a probability above 0.5. This can be explained by the pseudo-labels: 
			a positive bag contains on average 54 positive patches, but with k = 8 only the eight patches with the 
			highest attention are used as positive examples, so the patch classifier only learns to recognise the 
			most obvious wheat ears. To improve the recall, the next step is to increase k.

			\subsubsection{Increasing the number of pseudo-labels}\label{sec:clam_k_opt}
			A larger k marks more patches as positive in each positive bag. It should
			therefore cover more of the actually positive patches. In bags with few positive patches,
			the top k patches inevitably contain background noise. A sweep of k values from 4 to 32 was therefore performed.
			The lower limit of 4 was added to check the effect of a smaller number of pseudo-labels. Additionally the bag weight was 
			varied over the values 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9. The sweep for $w = 0.3$ was done in a separate experiment and only ran to k = 20. 
			The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:clam_k_sweep}.

			\begin{figure}
				\centering
				\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/clam_k_sweep.png}
				\caption[Top-k pseudo-labels: patch \acs*{auc}, counting \acs*{mae}, precision and recall for a sweep over k with one line per bag weight]{Top-k pseudo-labels: patch \ac{auc}, counting \ac{mae}, precision and recall for a sweep over 
				k with one line per bag weight. The default configuration uses k = 8 and bag weight = 0.7.
				The counting \ac{mae} is calculated for the calibrated counting rule.}
				\label{fig:clam_k_sweep}
			\end{figure}

			The recall increases monotonically with
			k for every bag weight. At a bag weight of 0.7, it rises from 0.208 (k = 8) to 0.381 (k = 20) and 0.465
			(k = 32). Compared with the default configuration, it is higher at k = 20 (3/3, 0.144). At $w = 0.9$, the recall remains below that of the
			other bag weights up to k = 20. At k = 8, it reaches 0.125 there, while for the others it ranges from 0.208 to
			0.236. From k = 24 onwards, the curves for the three bag weights, based on runs in this range,
			converge at values between 0.40 and 0.47. This is an expected observation, as the patch classifier is trained on more
			positive patches and therefore learns to classify more of them as positive. The bag weight has no real influence.

			The precision follows the opposite trend, showing a downward trend for all bag weights, except for $w = 0.9$ on low k values.
			It remains the most constant for $w = 0.7$ between 0.783 and 0.810 for k ranging from 8 to 24. At k = 28, it falls to 0.754, and at k = 32
			to 0.740. For $w = 0.3$ and $w = 0.5$, it decreases steadily with k, from around 0.85 at k = 4
			to between 0.76 and 0.77. The lowest values are reached for $w = 0.9$, with a range of 0.71 to 0.76 between k of 4 and 12. 
			The behaviour of the precision is expected, the patch classifier needs to become more broad in its definition of positive patches 
			when trained on a higher number of pseudo-labels. The high precision at low k for $w = 0.3$ and $0.5$
			show the focus of the model on the most obvious positive patches, especially when considering the low recall at the same time.

			Overall, the relationship between precision and recall is a trade-off. The model can either focus on the most obvious positive patches, 
			missing most of the others, or it can learn to classify more patches as positive, at the cost of misclassifying negative patches. Only considering these
			two metrics, the best configuration is $w = 0.7$ with k = 20 or 24. 

			The patch \ac{auc} is not as clear. The same monotonic increase with k as for the recall can be observed, with a saturating effect from k = 20 
			onwards. After k = 20, all median values lie between 0.723 and 0.766. This span of 0.043 which shows a smaller dispersion than between 
			the seeds of the same configuration (0.047). Therefore, an increase of k beyond 20 does not improve the patch \ac{auc} significantly. 
			The best patch \ac{auc} of 0.766 is reached for w = 0.5 and k = 32, while w = 0.9 stays above 0.710 for all k. The other 
			bag weights struggle especially for low k values. Over all k values the median patch \ac{auc} spans a range of 0.137.
			The metric resolves the differences between low and high k values. Compared with the default configuration, the patch \ac{auc} increases from 
			0.701 to 0.755 when w = 0.7 (k = 20).

			Considering the bag weight, when k = 4, the patch \ac{auc} rises from 0.629 (w = 0.3) to 0.710
			(w = 0.9). When k is small, a high bag weight therefore aids separation. However, it reduces
			recall and precision in this case. With w = 0.3, the bag accuracy ranges from 0.745 to
			0.820. A strong weight on the patch task therefore comes at the expense of
			Bag accuracy. The pseudo-labels originate from the attention mechanism of the Bag branch. A
			weaker Bag branch is therefore likely to adversely affect the pseudo-labels as well.
			
			The Bag-\acs*{auc} ranges from 0.910 to 0.958 across all configurations.
			The Bag-Accuracy ranges from 0.745 to 0.905. Median values show that 4 of the 33
			configurations reach the acceptance threshold of 0.90. All four have $k \geq 24$.
			The median recall at the bag level is 0.910, while the precision is 0.832. The misclassification 
			is therefore leaning towards false positives: empty bags are passed to the counting step and could receive a count higher than 0.
			That would inflate the counting error, but if the patch classifier is able to classify the patches correctly, the counting error should not be impacted.
			This problem could probably be solved by using a different threshold for the bag classification, but this was not tested in this thesis.
			Although most of the configuration missed the acceptance threshold, the margin is low and the bag-\acs*{auc} is still high. 
			Therefore, with the patch-level task not being impacted too greatly, this problem will not be further investigated.

			Based on all the results k = 20 with w = 0.7 was chosen as the best configuration going forward. When k = 20,
			the patch \ac{auc} reaches its plateau. Of the four bag weights, w = 0.7 yields the
			highest patch \ac{auc}, precision and bag accuracy at this point. Compared with the default configuration,
			recall increases from 0.208 to 0.381. Precision, however, decreases from 0.810 to
			0.784. A larger k further increases recall but reduces precision. The bag accuracy
			stands at 0.890, just below the acceptance threshold. The configuration k = 32,
			w = 0.7 achieves 0.905, with the same patch \ac{auc} (0.753) and lower
			precision (0.740). Additionally, this configuration has 
			the lowest counting \ac{mae} when excluding the configurations with w = 0.9, which are overall outlier.
			The following experiments use k = 20 with w = 0.7.

			\subsubsection{Counting performance based on the counting rule}\label{sec:clam_thresholding}
			At this point the patch separation performance seems to be saturated for the \acs*{clam}-based model. Therefore, this is the point 
			in the development in this thesis where the focus will shift from the patch separation to the counting performance, following 
			the original main goal of this work.

			The \acs*{clam}-based model provides probabilities for each patch. Different counting rules can therefore
			be applied to the same trained model. Five counting rules were investigated:
			\begin{itemize}
				\item \textbf{Fixed} --- $1/K$, the counting rule of the \ac{abmil} model for comparison and 0.5, which is the default threshold for a binary decision.
				\item \textbf{Otsu} --- individual thresholds for each bag.
				\item \textbf{Calibrated} --- a global threshold calibrated by minimising \ac{mae} on the validation split.
				\item \textbf{Soft-count} --- the sum of the patch-probabilities without threshold.
				\item \textbf{Baseline} --- a predetermined proportion of the bag size: $p_\text{train} * K$, with $p_\text{train} = 0.25$
					as the average proportion of positive patches per trainings bag.
			\end{itemize}

			Since all rules are applied to the same trained models, differences between them can only come 
			from the counting rule and not from training noise. They are compared for the two selected 
			configurations and the default configuration as a reference.

			Important to note is that the four counting rules only count in bags classified as positive, 
			while the baseline counts in every bag. For k = 20, a bag recall of 0.900 and a 
			bag precision of 0.882 mean that about 10 positive bags are missed and about 12 
			empty bags receive a count; for the quantile variant (0.900 and 0.861) it is about 
			10 and 15 bags. The reference point for all counting errors is the ‘always zero’ predictor with
			\ac{mae} = 27.06.

			11.89. These were obtained without calibration, using a fixed threshold or the Otsu threshold. The
			difference from 9.46 is therefore due to a combination of the model and the selection process. Under the uncalibrated rule
			1/K, the selected Top-k configuration achieves 11.42. This falls within the range of the
			Attention model. The \ac{clam} model provides, with the probability per patch, a
			value against which a global threshold can be calibrated. In \ac{abmil} model,
			the weights per bag are normalised. A global threshold has no fixed meaning there.
			Calibration for \ac{abmil} model was not investigated.

			\subsubsection{Interim conclusion}
			Already with the default values of \ac{clam}, the patch branch raises the patch \ac{auc} 
			above the limit of the \ac{abmil} model, but with a low recall on the patch level. 
			Increasing k sample improves the recall with only a small loss of precision, 
			and the patch \ac{auc} rises up to about k = 20 before it levels off; at this point, 
			the bag weight of 0.7 gives the best separation, precision and bag accuracy. 
			The quantile-based pseudo-labels shift the operating point further towards recall 
			without improving the separation. For the following experiments, Top-k with k = 20 
			and w = 0.7 and the quantile variant 0.80/0.10 were selected, although none of them 
			reaches the 0.909 of the supervised patch classifier.

			For counting, the threshold calibrated on the validation split is the best rule for 
			both configurations. With it the counting error follows the patch separability. 
			The result of the fixed threshold 1/K and 0.5 mainly depends on the scale 
			of the patch probabilities, which changes with the pseudo-labelling mode. 0.5 undercounts continuously, 
			but is reduced with increasing k, which matches the increasing recall. But even with 
			the modified pseudo-labeling methods, there is still a big group of positive patches that are scored even below 
			the 1/K threshold. It seems not to be possible to reach those patches with the current method.
			

			\subsection{Pretrained backbone}\label{sec:pretrain_backbone}
			Even with the improvements of the \acs*{clam}-based model, the best patch \ac{auc} of 0.755 is 
			still clearly below the 0.909 of the supervised classifier with the same backbone, 
			so the backbone is able to learn separable features when patch labels are available.
			This section therefore tests whether the \ac{mil} models 
			profit enough from a pretrained backbone to justify the additional annotation effort.

			For this purpose, a patch classifier with the default backbone architecture was trained like the supervised classifier in Section~\ref{sec:gwhd_reference}
			on 10,000 labelled training patches from the centred \ac{gwhd} bags, over 50
			epochs. Its backbone weights, without the classification head, were then used to initialise the backbone 
			of the \ac{mil} models, which was trained further with a learning rate reduced by a 
			factor of 100, while the rest of the model used the normal learning rate.

			The three models selected so far were tested: \ac{abmil} with
			softmax and the two \ac{clam} configurations from previous section. Each
			configuration was run once with standard initialisation and once with a pretrained
			backbone, in each case with 3 seeds. The pairs differ in terms of
			initialisation, the backbone’s learning rate and weight decay. The dataset is the centred
			\ac{gwhd} dataset with a reference error of 27.06.

			\subsubsection{Interim conclusion}
			On the synthetic drone bags the method works as long as the patches are clearly separable, 
			and the remaining error comes from the counting rule. On the real drone images the bag task is 
			easier than on \ac{gwhd}, but the patch level remains the bottleneck, with a patch \ac{auc} of 0.533 
			for \ac{abmil} and 0.636 for Top-k, both below the \ac{gwhd} values with the same backbone, which might 
			partly be caused by label noise. The pretrained backbone shows the same pattern as on \ac{gwhd} and 
			additionally leads to stable training for the quantile variant. The greedy search selects 256 feature 
			maps for Top-k, where this configuration is both the most stable and the one with the highest patch \ac{auc}, 
			while no stable configuration was found for the quantile variant.

		\subsection{From patch to wheat ear counts}\label{sec:img_infer}
		All previous sections evaluated the counting of positive patches as the intermediate step 
		towards counting wheat ears. This section completes the pipeline by converting the patch 
		counts into ear counts on the point-annotated drone images (Drone\_annot, 83/20/70 images). The pipeline
		consists of 3 steps:

		\begin{itemize}
			\item \ac{clam} evaluation on bags from the annotated drone images, with three patch sizes (32, 64, 128 px)
			\item the same trained models are then evaluated on the patch level for full images
			\item finally, the positive patch count is converted to an ear count using a linear and isotonic regression function
		\end{itemize}

		linear conversion and in all six for the soft count with isotonic conversion, and better than 
		32 px in all six pairs. The Spearman correlation is also higher at 64 px (5/6, median 0.121), although 
		the patch \ac{auc} is lower than at 128 px in every pair (median 0.085). The patch size with the highest 
		patch \ac{auc} is therefore not the best one for counting ears.

		and a positive patch contains on average 6.87 ears (up to 22). In dense images almost every patch is positive, 
		no matter if the image contains 400 or 600 ears, so the number of positive patches saturates and the conversion 
		cannot recover the missing information, which is also where the bias is most negative (-61.25 and -67.59). 
		At 32 px a positive patch contains on average 1.17 ears and would therefore carry the counting information, 
		but the patch \ac{auc} of 0.729 and 0.744 is too low at this resolution, leading to \acp{mae} between 72.7 and 167.9 for 
		Top-k and between 135.0 and 154.9 for the quantile variant. With 2.14 ears per positive patch and a patch \ac{auc} 
		of 0.844 and 0.875, 64 px is the best compromise in these experiments.

		calibrated threshold was still better than 0.5 at 32 px in all six seeds. This shows that the threshold is 
		calibrated on the secondary goal, the number of positive patches per bag, and does not necessarily transfer 
		to the full images.

		18 models for both conversions, including all six models at 32 px. For the quantile variant with 64 px 
		the difference is small (51.91 compared to 52.05), but consistent over the three seeds.

		Between the two conversion functions no clear advantage can be seen. As both are monotonic, they 
		do not change the Spearman correlation, and the isotonic function is better than the linear 
		one in 11 of 18 models for the hard count and in 13 of 18 for the soft count, while for 
		the quantile variant with 64 px the linear function is better in two of three seeds. A 
		disadvantage of the isotonic function is that it cannot return values above the highest 
		count in the validation images, which affects the 16 test images with more than 475 ears.

		The bias of the linear conversion is negative for all 18 models, so the converted count is 
		systematically too low. For the quantile variant with 64 px it is -36.97 (-59.52 to -36.63), 
		about 10 \% of the mean number of ears. A likely reason is that the conversion was fitted on 
		the images from the validation split that have a lower average of ears per image and a lower maximum ear count.

		Based on the patch \ac{auc} alone, 128 px would have been selected, while for counting ears 64 px is the 
		better choice. The patch \ac{auc} therefore only works as a selection criterion within one patch size: 
		at 64 px the quantile variant has the higher patch \ac{auc} (0.875 compared to 0.844) and also counts 
		better, both in two of three seeds. Between patch sizes it compares different tasks and cannot 
		be used to select the patch size.

		In summary, the patch separation transfers from the bags to the full images, and the best ear 
		count is reached by the quantile variant 0.80/0.10 with 64-px patches. Larger patches are easier 
		to separate but contain too many ears, while smaller patches would carry the counting information 
		but are not separated well enough. The threshold calibrated on the bags does not improve the ear 
		count compared to 0.5, and the soft count is better than the calibrated hard count in most models.

		These results have several limitations. The patch size was selected on the test split, and three 
		seeds are not enough to resolve small differences. The conversion is fitted on only 20 images and 
		the counting threshold is calibrated on the bags and not on the full images.

		\section{Conclusion and Outlook}
		\subsection{Conclusion}
			The aim of this thesis was to find out whether wheat ears in drone images can be counted with a model 
			that is trained only on binary bag labels, and how far such a weak signal can support counting. 
			For this purpose, attention-based MIL and a CLAM-based extension were adapted to count positive patches 
			by thresholding attention weights or patch probabilities, and the resulting patch counts were converted 
			into ear counts. The method was developed step by step on MNIST, the GWHD dataset, synthetic drone images 
			and finally on real drone images from two years.

			The experiments on MNIST and on the synthetic drone images show that the basic idea works as long as the 
			positive and negative patches can be separated. On both datasets the patch AUC lies above 0.92, and the 
			remaining counting error mainly comes from the counting rule. With softmax attention and the fixed threshold 
			of $1/K$, the error is almost entirely a systematic undercount, as the normalisation of the weights to a sum 
			of one concentrates the attention on a few patches and leaves more positive patches below the threshold the 
			more of them a bag contains. This was shown most clearly on the high-density MNIST bags, where softmax 
			separates the patches better than sigmoid (0.928 compared to 0.857) but counts clearly worse 
			(MAE 10.98 compared to 6.61).

			On real wheat images, the separation of the patches is the main bottleneck. On the GWHD bags the bag 
			classification is saturated with a bag AUC of about 0.95, while the patch AUC of the AbMIL model stays 
			between 0.406 and 0.655 for all tested changes. These include gated attention, five activation functions, 
			four backbone modifications, a greedy search over the backbone dimensions, a lower density of positive 
			patches and colour input. The supervised patch classifier with the same backbone reaches 0.909, so the 
			patches can be separated, but the bag label alone does not force the model to learn this separation. 
			The bag decision can already be made from the overall texture of a bag, and once it is solved, no further 
			learning signal reaches the patch level. The changes to the model mainly shifted the counting error through 
			the interaction between the shape of the attention distribution and the threshold, without improving the 
			separation itself.

			The additional patch-level task of the CLAM-based model, trained on pseudo-labels from the attention weights, 
			raised the separation above the range of the AbMIL model without using patch labels, with a patch AUC of 0.755 
			on GWHD (Top-k with $k = 20$). As the patch classifier returns probabilities that are comparable across bags, 
			a global threshold can be calibrated on the validation split. This reduced the counting error of positive 
			patches to 9.46, compared to 27.06 for the always-zero predictor. With a calibrated threshold, the counting 
			error also follows the patch separability, so the patch AUC proved to be a suitable selection criterion within 
			one patch definition. A backbone pretrained with patch labels improved the separation further, but did not 
			close the gap to the supervised classifier and was therefore not included in the method.

			On the real drone images the bag task is easier than on GWHD, but the patch level remains the limiting factor, 
			and the training becomes unstable, especially for the quantile-based pseudo-labels many seeds classify the bags significantly worse than the others. 
			For the final ear counting on the annotated drone images from 2025, the patch size turned out to be the most 
			important parameter. Larger patches are easier to separate (patch AUC up to 0.957 at 128\,px), but contain too 
			many ears, so the number of positive patches saturates in dense images. 32-px patches would carry the counting 
			information, but are not separated well enough. With 64-px patches, the quantile variant reaches an MAE of 
			52.05 ears per image (51.91 with the soft count), compared to 132.67 for the mean predictor. This corresponds 
			to about 15\,\% of the mean number of ears, with an $R^2$ of 0.836 and a Spearman correlation of 0.852. 
			The converted counts are systematically too low, but the ranking of the images is preserved.

			Overall, the proposed method is able to count wheat ears to some degree, using only binary bag labels for 
			training and 20 annotated images for the conversion, but it does not reach a performance that would be 
			sufficient for practical use. The result is further limited by the experimental setup. The training bags were 
			sampled from patch pools that were defined using the annotations, the patch size and most configurations were 
			selected on the test split, and three seeds are not enough to resolve small differences.

		\subsection{Outlook}
			The limitations of the results suggest several experiments and changes that could be tested in future work.

			The next step would be to train the models on bags that do not depend on any annotation. The drone images 
			from 2026, taken before and after the ears became visible, already provide such bags, and the models trained 
			on them could be evaluated directly on the annotated images from 2025, which would also test the transfer 
			between two years. Using whole images as bags instead of randomly sampled patches would additionally show 
			whether the method still works when the share of positive patches is given by the field and not by the 
			sampling.

			A second direction is a stronger but still cheap learning signal. The binary bag label saturates after 
			the first positive patch (Section~\ref{sec:method_wheat_mil}), which was identified as the main reason for the weak patch separation. 
			Bag labels that give the approximate number of ears or the share of positive patches, as in the count-based 
			MIL approaches mentioned in Section 2.4, would keep the annotation effort far below that of point annotations 
			while providing a signal that does not saturate.

			Since the pretrained backbone improved the separation and stabilised the training on the drone images, better 
			features seem to be the most promising approach on the model side. To stay within weak supervision, the 
			backbone could be pretrained without patch labels, for example with ImageNet weights as in the original CLAM 
			or with self-supervised pretraining on the large number of unlabelled drone images. In the same context, 
			colour input could be tested again with a larger backbone. The reasons for the collapsing and unstable seeds should also be 
			investigated, for example by testing different learning rates, initialisations or a different balance of the 
			pseudo-labels.

			For the conversion to ear counts, the patch definition could be refined. Patch sizes between 32 and 64\,px or 
			overlapping patches could improve the trade-off between separability and the number of ears per patch, and 
			instead of only counting positive patches, the conversion could use the patch probabilities directly or 
			estimate the number of ears per positive patch. The conversion functions should be fitted on images from 
			several flights that cover the full range of densities, as the isotonic function cannot return values above 
			the highest validation count and the linear conversion was biased by the lower density of the validation 
			images. Calibrating the counting threshold on full images instead of bags could also be tested.

			Finally, the evaluation should be made more robust. An evaluation on images from other flights, years or 
			locations, and on the official GWHD splits, would show how well the method handles domain shifts, which is 
			where the first experiments on GWHD failed. A fully supervised method trained on the same images would put the 
			remaining error into context and show how much of it is caused by the weak supervision.
